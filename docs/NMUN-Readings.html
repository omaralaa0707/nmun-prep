UN foundations

The League of Nations (1920–1946)

About 16 min

The League was the first permanent international organization built to keep peace. Interviewers ask about it mainly to see whether you understand why the UN was designed the way it was.

Key facts

  • Origin: the last of US President Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points (January 1918) called for "a general association of nations".
  • Founding document: the Covenant of the League of Nations, Part I of the Treaty of Versailles, signed 28 June 1919 at the Paris Peace Conference.
  • In force: 10 January 1920, with 42 founding members. Headquarters: Geneva.
  • United States never joined. The Senate refused to ratify Versailles, fearing entanglement in foreign wars (isolationism).
  • Permanent Council members at founding: UK, France, Italy, Japan. Germany joined 1926 and left 1933; the USSR joined 1934 and was expelled in 1939 for invading Finland.
  • Peak membership 58 states (1934–35); 63 states were members at some point. Egypt joined in 1937, the last state to join.
  • Dissolved itself at its final Assembly in April 1946, transferring assets to the UN.

Why it failed

  • Unanimity rule: Assembly and Council decisions generally needed every member's agreement, so one state could block action.
  • Missing great powers: no US ever; Germany, Japan and Italy left in the 1930s; the USSR was expelled.
  • No enforcement: no army of its own and weak sanctions.
  • Manchuria (1931): Japan invaded; the Lytton Report took a year and condemned Japan, which simply left the League in 1933.
  • Abyssinia (1935–36): Italy invaded Ethiopia; sanctions excluded oil and Britain and France kept the Suez Canal open, so they failed.
  • Appeasement: the Munich Agreement (1938) let Germany take the Sudetenland; Germany invaded the rest of Czechoslovakia (March 1939) and Poland (1 September 1939). War broke out.

What survived

The ILO (1919) became the first UN specialized agency; the Permanent Court of International Justice became the ICJ; the mandates system became the Trusteeship system. Successes worth one line: the Åland Islands dispute (1921) and the Nansen passport for refugees.

In depth

From war to Covenant

  • 8 January 1918: Wilson presents his Fourteen Points to Congress. Point 14 calls for "a general association of nations" to guarantee political independence and territorial integrity "to great and small states alike".
  • 11 November 1918: armistice signed in a railway carriage in the forest of Compiègne, France. Fighting stops.
  • 18 January 1919: the Paris Peace Conference opens. A commission chaired by Wilson drafts the Covenant; the conference adopts it on 28 April 1919.
  • 28 June 1919: the Treaty of Versailles is signed; the Covenant is its Part I. Germany must pay reparations, disarm, lose territory and give up its colonies. The harshness of these terms fed German resentment that Hitler later exploited.
  • November 1919 and March 1920: the US Senate twice rejects Versailles. Republicans led by Henry Cabot Lodge feared Article 10 (collective defence of members' territory) would drag the US into wars without Congress's approval; Wilson, weakened by a stroke, refused to compromise.

The mandates system placed colonies taken from the defeated powers under "advanced nations": Class A (Middle East: Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Palestine, Transjordan) were closest to independence; Class B (central Africa: Tanganyika, Cameroons, Togoland, Ruanda-Urundi); Class C (South West Africa and Pacific islands) were run almost as colonies. The UN turned most remaining mandates into Trust Territories; South West Africa (Namibia) only became independent in 1990.

Successes worth naming

  • Åland Islands (1921): awarded to Finland with autonomy for the Swedish-speaking population; Sweden accepted.
  • Upper Silesia (1921) partitioned between Germany and Poland after a plebiscite.
  • Greece–Bulgaria (1925): the League stopped a border war and Greece paid compensation.
  • Refugees: the Nansen passport (1922) gave stateless refugees travel documents; Fridtjof Nansen won the 1922 Nobel Peace Prize.
  • Social work: the 1926 Slavery Convention, campaigns against leprosy and malaria, drug trafficking controls.

In depth

The wartime declarations in detail

  • London (St James's Palace) Declaration, 12 June 1941: the UK, the Commonwealth dominions and the governments-in-exile of occupied Europe (Belgium, Czechoslovakia, Greece, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Yugoslavia, and de Gaulle's Free France) pledged to work for "a world in which, relieved of the menace of aggression, all may enjoy economic and social security".
  • Atlantic Charter, 14 August 1941: a joint statement by Roosevelt and Churchill after meeting aboard warships in Placentia Bay, Newfoundland (the US was not yet at war). Its eight points: no territorial gains; no territorial changes against the wishes of the people; self-determination; equal access to trade and raw materials; economic cooperation; freedom from fear and want; freedom of the seas; and abandoning the use of force, with disarmament of aggressors.
  • Declaration by United Nations, 1 January 1942: signed in Washington by 26 states, led by the US, UK, USSR and China. They pledged to use full resources against the Axis, not to make a separate peace, and to uphold the Atlantic Charter. By 1945, 47 states had signed; signing (and declaring war on the Axis) became the ticket to the San Francisco Conference.
  • Moscow Conference, October 1943: foreign ministers of the US, UK and USSR, with China joining for one text, issued four declarations: on General Security (the first commitment to a "general international organization… for the maintenance of international peace and security"), on Italy, on Austria, and on Atrocities.
  • Tehran Conference, 28 November–1 December 1943: the first meeting of Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin; mainly war strategy, but Roosevelt outlined his "Four Policemen" idea for the post-war body.

Dumbarton Oaks and Yalta

Dumbarton Oaks (a mansion in Washington, 21 August–7 October 1944) ran in two phases: first the US, UK and USSR, then the US, UK and China (the Soviets would not sit with China because the USSR was not yet at war with Japan). The "Proposals" created the four main bodies (a General Assembly with an Economic and Social Council under it, a Security Council, a court and a Secretariat) and said members would place armed forces at the Council's disposal, because the League's lack of force had been fatal. Two questions were left open: voting in the Security Council and the Soviet demand for a seat for each of its 16 republics.

Yalta (Crimea, 4–11 February 1945) settled both: the veto formula (unanimity of the permanent members on substantive matters, but not on procedure or when they are party to a dispute under Chapter VI), and two extra seats for the USSR (Ukraine and Byelorussia became original members). It also fixed the date and place of the founding conference and agreed the principle of trusteeship.

San Francisco

  • The United Nations Conference on International Organization (UNCIO), 25 April–26 June 1945, opened two weeks after Roosevelt's death. 50 states, about 850 delegates; the four sponsoring powers (US, UK, USSR, China) took turns chairing plenary meetings.
  • Smaller and middle powers (Australia, Canada, Latin American states) won changes: a stronger General Assembly, the Economic and Social Council as a principal organ, the human rights language, regional arrangements (Chapter VIII) and the right of collective self-defence in Article 51.
  • The Charter and the ICJ Statute were signed on 26 June 1945. Under Article 110, they entered into force once China, France, the USSR, the UK, the US and a majority of other signatories had ratified: 24 October 1945.
  • Poland, whose government had not been agreed among the Allies, signed on 15 October 1945 as the 51st original member.

Getting started

  • A Preparatory Commission in London organized the first meetings.
  • First General Assembly: 10 January 1946, Methodist Central Hall, Westminster; its first President was Paul-Henri Spaak of Belgium. Its first resolution (January 1946) set up a commission on atomic energy.
  • First Security Council meeting: 17 January 1946, Church House, London.
  • Trygve Lie (Norway) appointed Secretary-General on 1 February 1946.
  • Headquarters: temporarily at Lake Success, New York. John D. Rockefeller Jr. gave $8.5 million to buy the Manhattan site on the East River (December 1946). The Secretariat building was occupied in 1951 and the GA first met in its new hall on 14 October 1952.

The UN Charter

About 18 min

The Charter is the UN's founding treaty: 19 chapters, 111 articles, plus the annexed Statute of the ICJ (70 articles). It binds every member. Under Article 103, if a member's obligations under the Charter conflict with any other treaty, the Charter prevails.

Purposes (Article 1)

  1. Maintain international peace and security.
  2. Develop friendly relations based on equal rights and self-determination of peoples.
  3. Achieve international cooperation on economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems and promote human rights.
  4. Be a centre for harmonizing the actions of nations.

Principles (Article 2): the ones you will cite

  • 2(1) sovereign equality of all members.
  • 2(3) settle disputes by peaceful means.
  • 2(4) refrain from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state.
  • 2(7) the UN may not intervene in matters essentially within a state's domestic jurisdiction, except enforcement measures under Chapter VII.

The five amendments

  • 1965: Art. 23 enlarged the Security Council from 11 to 15; Art. 27 raised the voting threshold from 7 to 9; Art. 61 enlarged ECOSOC from 18 to 27.
  • 1968: Art. 109 adjusted to the larger Council (a review conference can be called by any nine SC members).
  • 1973: Art. 61 enlarged ECOSOC from 27 to 54.

Authentic texts (Art. 111): Chinese, French, Russian, English, Spanish. Arabic became an official language of the GA in 1973, making six official languages today.

In depth

The Preamble

It opens with "We the peoples of the United Nations", not "we the states", and states four determinations: to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war; to reaffirm faith in fundamental human rights, the dignity and worth of the human person and the equal rights of men and women and of nations large and small; to establish conditions for justice and respect for international law; and to promote social progress and better standards of life in larger freedom. "In Larger Freedom" became the title of Kofi Annan's 2005 reform report.

All seven principles of Article 2

  1. Sovereign equality of all members.
  2. Fulfil Charter obligations in good faith.
  3. Settle disputes by peaceful means.
  4. Refrain from the threat or use of force against any state's territorial integrity or political independence.
  5. Assist the UN in any action under the Charter and refrain from helping a state the UN is acting against.
  6. Ensure that non-member states act in line with these principles as far as necessary for peace and security.
  7. No UN intervention in matters essentially within domestic jurisdiction, but this does not prejudice Chapter VII enforcement.

How the amendments were made

All five amendments came from the wave of decolonization: membership grew from 51 (1945) to 117 (1965) and 135 (1973), so the Security Council and ECOSOC had to grow to keep regional representation fair. They were adopted by the GA (the 1963 resolutions entered into force in 1965) and ratified by two-thirds of members including all permanent members. No amendment has been adopted since 1973. The phrase "Republic of China" and "Union of Soviet Socialist Republics" in Article 23 has never been changed; the seats are held by the PRC (since 1971) and the Russian Federation (since December 1991, as continuator state of the USSR, accepted without a vote).

Membership, observers and non-members

About 15 min

Numbers

  • 193 member states. The newest is South Sudan (14 July 2011).
  • Two non-member observer states: the Holy See (observer since 1964) and the State of Palestine (non-member observer state since GA resolution A/RES/67/19, 2012).
  • In May 2024 the GA (resolution A/RES/ES-10/23) found Palestine qualified for membership and gave it extra rights from September 2024 (it sits among members in alphabetical order and can submit proposals), but it still cannot vote. Full membership needs a Security Council recommendation, which the US vetoed in April 2024.
  • Over 150 member states recognize Palestine, including the UK, France, Canada and Australia, which did so in September 2025.

How a state joins (Article 4)

  1. The state applies to the Secretary-General with a declaration accepting Charter obligations.
  2. The Security Council recommends admission: 9 votes and no P5 veto.
  3. The General Assembly admits it by a two-thirds majority.

The UN does not "recognize" states; only states recognize other states. The UN admits members and accepts the credentials of governments (checked by the GA's Credentials Committee).

Losing rights

  • Article 5: suspension of rights when the SC takes enforcement action against a member. Never formally used.
  • Article 6: expulsion for persistent violation of Charter principles. Never used.
  • Article 19: loss of GA vote for arrears of two full years. Applied regularly; states can get an exemption if non-payment is beyond their control.
  • Credentials rejected: South Africa's delegation was barred from the GA in 1974 over apartheid and returned in 1994. This was a credentials decision, not a suspension of membership.
  • Yugoslavia (1992): the GA decided the FRY could not automatically continue the old Yugoslavia's seat; it was admitted as a new member in 2000.
  • Indonesia announced its withdrawal in 1965 (protesting Malaysia's SC seat) and returned in 1966; the only state ever to try to leave.
  • Human Rights Council membership can be suspended by a two-thirds GA vote: Libya (2011) and Russia (2022).

Disputed entities in more detail

Taiwan. The Republic of China was a founding member with a permanent SC seat. After losing the civil war in 1949, the ROC government moved to Taiwan while the People's Republic controlled the mainland. For two decades the ROC kept China's seat, backed by the US. By 1971 the PRC had wide recognition, and A/RES/2758 (XXVI) (25 October 1971) recognized the PRC's representatives as "the only lawful representatives of China" and expelled "the representatives of Chiang Kai-shek". Taiwan's attempts to return (1993–2008, latterly as "Taiwan") were rejected by the GA's General Committee. Only around a dozen states recognize it; the PRC's "One China" principle makes recognition of Taiwan incompatible with relations with Beijing.

Kosovo. After NATO's 1999 campaign against Serbia, Kosovo was placed under the UN Interim Administration Mission in Kosovo (UNMIK) by S/RES/1244. It declared independence on 17 February 2008. Around 100+ states recognize it (counts are disputed because some have withdrawn recognition). The US, UK and France support it; Russia and China, with Serbia, oppose it, so it cannot pass the SC. On 22 July 2010 the ICJ advised that the declaration "did not violate international law", because international law contains no prohibition on declarations of independence. Kosovo is a member of the IMF and World Bank but not the UN.

Somaliland. Former British Somaliland was briefly independent (26 June 1960) before uniting with Italian Somalia on 1 July 1960. After the Somali state collapsed, Somaliland declared independence in 1991 and has held elections, its own currency and army since. It lacks broad recognition because (1) Somalia's federal government claims it; (2) the AU defends colonial-era borders (uti possidetis) and fears setting a precedent for secession across Africa; (3) admission needs SC approval. In January 2024 Ethiopia signed a memorandum of understanding with Somaliland for sea access, which Somalia called a violation of its sovereignty.

Western Sahara. A Spanish colony until 1975. The ICJ (advisory opinion, 16 October 1975) found historical ties to Morocco and Mauritania but no tie of territorial sovereignty, so self-determination applied. Morocco's Green March (November 1975) and the Madrid Accords transferred administration to Morocco and Mauritania. The Polisario Front proclaimed the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic (SADR) in 1976 and fought both; Mauritania withdrew in 1979. A UN-brokered ceasefire in 1991 created MINURSO to organize a referendum, which never took place because the parties disagreed on voter eligibility. Morocco proposed autonomy under its sovereignty in 2007. The ceasefire broke down in November 2020; in December 2020 the US recognized Moroccan sovereignty, and Spain (2022), France (2024) and the UK (2025) backed the autonomy plan. Algeria backs Polisario. In October 2025 the SC backed autonomy as the basis for negotiations (check the latest MINURSO resolution).

Northern Cyprus. After a Greek-backed coup and Türkiye's military intervention in 1974, the island was divided. The "Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus" was declared in 1983; S/RES/541 (1983) called the declaration legally invalid. Only Türkiye recognizes it. UNFICYP has kept the buffer zone since 1964.

Credentials, suspension and arrears in practice

  • The GA's Credentials Committee decides which government's delegates sit for a state. Contested cases: China (until 1971), Cambodia (Khmer Rouge kept the seat into the 1980s), South Africa (1974), and since 2021 Afghanistan (Taliban) and Myanmar (military government), where decisions have been deferred so the previous governments' representatives keep the seats.
  • Yugoslavia: in 1992 (A/RES/47/1) the GA decided the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia could not automatically continue the old Yugoslavia's membership and must apply anew; it was admitted in 2000 (later Serbia and Montenegro, then Serbia).
  • Article 19 is applied every year: the Secretary-General notifies the GA of members in arrears, and the GA sometimes grants exemptions when non-payment is beyond a state's control.

The UN system, money and current agenda

Six principal organs (Article 7)

General Assembly, Security Council, Economic and Social Council, Trusteeship Council (inactive since 1994), International Court of Justice, Secretariat. Five are in New York; the ICJ sits in The Hague.

Principal organs

The General Assembly and its six committees

About 20 min

The GA is the UN's main deliberative, policymaking and representative organ (Chapter IV, Articles 9–22). All 193 members sit in it, one state one vote.

Powers and functions

  • Discuss any question within the Charter (Art. 10) and make recommendations, which are not legally binding.
  • Exception in Art. 12: it should not recommend on a dispute the Security Council is actively handling.
  • Approves the budget and the scale of assessments (Art. 17). This is binding.
  • Elects the 10 non-permanent SC members, the 54 ECOSOC members and the 47 HRC members; with the SC, elects ICJ judges.
  • Appoints the Secretary-General on the SC's recommendation and admits new members on the SC's recommendation.
  • Initiates studies and codification of international law (Art. 13), mostly through the International Law Commission.
  • Considers reports from the SC and other organs.

Voting (Art. 18)

Important questions (peace and security recommendations, elections, admission, budget) need two-thirds of members present and voting. Other questions need a simple majority. In practice, most resolutions are adopted without a vote (by consensus).

Sessions and leadership

  • The regular session opens each September; the high-level general debate follows a week later. The 81st session opened 8 September 2026.
  • President of the GA (81st session): Khalilur Rahman of Bangladesh, elected 2 June 2026, after Annalena Baerbock (Germany) presided over the 80th. The presidency rotates among regional groups, one-year term.
  • The General Committee (President, 21 vice-presidents and the six Main Committee chairs) organizes the agenda; the Credentials Committee (9 members) checks delegations' credentials.
  • Special sessions and emergency special sessions can be called. Eleven emergency special sessions have been held, including ES-10 on Palestine (since 1997) and ES-11 on Ukraine (2022).

Bypassing the veto

  • Uniting for Peace (A/RES/377 (V), 3 November 1950, during the Korean War): if the SC fails to act because of a lack of P5 unanimity, the GA can meet in emergency special session within 24 hours and recommend collective measures. It can be triggered by a procedural SC vote (any nine members, no veto) or a majority of GA members. First used for the Suez Crisis (1956), which led to UNEF I.
  • Veto initiative (A/RES/76/262, April 2022, led by Liechtenstein): whenever a veto is cast, the GA meets within 10 working days to debate it.

Can GA resolutions become binding?

Not by themselves. But they can be evidence of customary international law when they reflect state practice and opinio juris (the belief that something is legally required). The UDHR is the classic example: many of its provisions are now considered customary law. GA decisions on internal matters (budget, elections, its own procedure) are binding.

The Security Council

About 21 min

The SC has primary responsibility for international peace and security (Art. 24). It is the only organ whose decisions bind all members (Art. 25). It covers Chapters V–VIII of the Charter (Arts. 23–54).

Membership

  • 15 members. Five permanent (P5): China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, the United States.
  • Ten elected (E10) by the GA by two-thirds for two-year terms, five each year, with no immediate re-election.
  • Regional seats: Africa 3, Asia-Pacific 2, Latin America and Caribbean 2, Western Europe and Others 2, Eastern Europe 1. One seat alternates between Asia-Pacific and Africa as an Arab seat.

Reform debate

Negotiated in the GA's Intergovernmental Negotiations (IGN). The G4 (Brazil, Germany, India, Japan) want new permanent seats; Uniting for Consensus (Italy, Pakistan, Argentina, Mexico, Republic of Korea and others) want more elected seats only; the African Union's Ezulwini Consensus asks for two permanent seats with the veto and five non-permanent seats for Africa. Any change needs a Charter amendment ratified by all the P5.

In depth

How the Council works day to day

  • Formats: public meetings (briefings and open debates, where non-members can speak under Rule 37), private meetings, and closed consultations where most negotiation happens. Arria-formula meetings are informal sessions convened by a member to hear civil society or other voices.
  • Penholders: one member drafts the texts on each situation. The US, UK and France hold most pens, which elected members criticize.
  • Presidency powers: the President for the month sets the programme of work, calls meetings (including at a member's or the Secretary-General's request), chairs, negotiates and reads presidential statements (adopted by consensus, published as S/PRST/…), issues press statements, and briefs non-members on the month's work.
  • Outcomes, from strongest to weakest: resolutions (S/RES/…), presidential statements, press statements, and letters.
  • Non-members directly affected may take part without a vote (Article 31), and parties to a dispute are invited (Article 32).

The veto in practice

  • The first veto was cast by the USSR in February 1946 (on troop withdrawals from Syria and Lebanon). The USSR cast most vetoes in the early Cold War, partly to block new members.
  • Since 1970 the US has vetoed more than any other member, many times on Israel–Palestine, including several Gaza ceasefire drafts since October 2023.
  • Russia (often with China) has vetoed repeatedly on Syria since 2011 and on Ukraine since 2022, including its own aggression (February 2022), which is why the veto initiative followed.
  • The UK and France have not used the veto since 1989.
  • A member that is a party to a dispute should abstain on Chapter VI decisions about it (Art. 27(3)), but this is rarely enforced.

ECOSOC and its commissions

About 15 min

The Economic and Social Council (Chapter X, Arts. 61–72) is the principal body for coordination, policy review and dialogue on economic, social and environmental issues, and for follow-up to the SDGs and major UN conferences.

Membership

  • 54 members, elected by the GA for three-year terms, 18 each year; immediate re-election allowed.
  • Seats: Africa 14, Asia-Pacific 11, Eastern Europe 6, Latin America and Caribbean 10, Western Europe and Others 13.
  • Enlarged from 18 to 27 (1965) and to 54 (1973) by Charter amendment.
  • Decisions by simple majority. The ECOSOC President serves one year.

Functions

  • Studies, reports and recommendations on economic, social, cultural, educational and health matters (Art. 62).
  • Prepares draft conventions for the GA; calls international conferences.
  • Coordinates specialized agencies through agreements with them (Arts. 57, 63).
  • Consultative status for NGOs (Art. 71): general, special or roster status, granted on the advice of the Committee on NGOs. Over 6,000 NGOs hold it.
  • Hosts the High-Level Political Forum on Sustainable Development (HLPF) every July, where states present Voluntary National Reviews (VNRs) of SDG progress. Every four years the HLPF meets under the GA at heads-of-state level (the SDG Summit; next in 2027).
  • Other segments: the Humanitarian Affairs Segment, the Youth Forum, the Financing for Development Forum, and the Operational Activities Segment on UN development work.
  • Its purview covers about 70% of the human and financial resources of the UN system.

Created in 1946, 53 members, Vienna. Why ECOSOC is criticized

Overlap with the GA Second and Third Committees, a sprawling subsidiary system, and little real authority over agencies that have their own governing bodies and budgets. Reforms (2013 and 2018 GA resolutions) refocused it on SDG follow-up, which is why the HLPF and Financing for Development Forum are now its centrepiece.

The International Court of Justice

About 16 min

The ICJ is the UN's principal judicial organ (Art. 92), sitting in the Peace Palace in The Hague. It replaced the League's Permanent Court of International Justice (PCIJ) in 1946. Its Statute is annexed to the Charter, and every UN member is automatically a party to it (Art. 93).

Judges

  • 15 judges, nine-year terms, a third elected every three years; re-election allowed.
  • Elected by an absolute majority in both the GA and the SC, voting separately. No veto applies.
  • No two judges of the same nationality; the bench should represent the main legal systems of the world.
  • A state in a case with no judge of its nationality may appoint a judge ad hoc.
  • President: Iwasawa Yuji (Japan) since March 2025, after Nawaf Salam (Lebanon) resigned to become Prime Minister of Lebanon.

How a case runs

  1. A state files an application (or both notify a special agreement).
  2. Requests for provisional measures are heard fast if there is urgency and a risk of irreparable harm.
  3. Preliminary objections: the respondent often challenges jurisdiction or admissibility first.
  4. Written phase (memorial, counter-memorial), then oral hearings in the Great Hall of Justice.
  5. Deliberation in private; the judgment is read in public. Judges can attach separate or dissenting opinions.

Cases often take years. If a party fails to comply, the other may go to the Security Council (Charter Art. 94(2)), where the veto can block enforcement, as with Nicaragua v. United States.

Correcting a common mistake

All 193 UN members are automatically parties to the ICJ Statute (Charter Art. 93). The US and Israel are parties; what they have not done is accept the Court's compulsory jurisdiction under the optional clause. A non-UN state can become a party to the Statute on conditions set by the GA and SC (Switzerland did before joining the UN). Kosovo is not a party. Palestine has appeared through a declaration accepting the Court's jurisdiction under SC resolution 9 (1946).

Vice-President: Julia Sebutinde (Uganda), alongside President Iwasawa Yuji. Five judges are elected every three years in November, by simultaneous but separate votes in the GA and SC.

The Secretariat and the Secretary-General

About 18 min

The Secretariat (Chapter XV, Arts. 97–101) carries out the day-to-day work mandated by the other organs: preparing reports, servicing meetings, running peace operations, mediating and gathering data. Its tens of thousands of staff are international civil servants who must not take instructions from any government (Art. 100).

The Secretary-General

  • "Chief administrative officer" of the UN (Art. 97).
  • Article 99: may bring to the SC's attention any matter that may threaten peace and security. Guterres formally invoked it over Gaza in December 2023, the first explicit use in decades.
  • Uses good offices: quiet diplomacy and mediation.
  • Presents the annual Report on the Work of the Organization to the GA.
  • Chairs the UN System Chief Executives Board for Coordination (CEB).

Selection

  1. Candidates are nominated by member states. Since 2016 candidates publish vision statements and face public dialogues in the GA (A/RES/69/321).
  2. The SC holds secret straw polls: "encourage", "discourage" or "no opinion", with P5 votes later colour-coded.
  3. The SC adopts a resolution recommending one candidate: 9 votes and no P5 veto.
  4. The GA appoints by resolution.

Term: five years, renewable; no one has served more than two terms. There has never been a woman Secretary-General; there is strong pressure for one in 2026. Informal regional rotation suggests Latin America and the Caribbean.

Geographical significance

  • Coastline of about 2,500 km on the Indian Ocean and the Mozambique Channel (between it and Madagascar), a major shipping lane between Asia and the Atlantic.
  • Six land neighbours: Tanzania (north), Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe (west), South Africa and Eswatini (south). Mnemonic: "TMZ-ZSE".
  • Gateway for landlocked neighbours. Its ports at Maputo, Beira and Nacala sit at the end of transport corridors for Malawi, Zambia and Zimbabwe. That makes Mozambique strategically important to all of southern Africa.
  • Great rivers: the Zambezi and Limpopo. The Cahora Bassa dam on the Zambezi exports electricity to South Africa.
  • Resources: the offshore Rovuma Basin gas fields off Cabo Delgado (among the largest in Africa), coal at Moatize (Tete), the world's largest ruby deposit at Montepuez, graphite at Balama, heavy sands, and the Mozal aluminium smelter.
  • Climate exposure: one of the most cyclone- and flood-prone countries in the world. Cyclones Idai and Kenneth (2019), Freddy (2023), and Chido (December 2024, hit Cabo Delgado), Dikeledi and Jude (2025). Disasters regularly displace hundreds of thousands, on top of the conflict.

History in six steps

  1. Portuguese colony from the 1500s. The Island of Mozambique was the capital until 1898 (UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1991).
  2. FRELIMO (Mozambique Liberation Front) founded in 1962 in Dar es Salaam under Eduardo Mondlane (assassinated 1969). Armed liberation struggle from 1964.
  3. Independence 25 June 1975 after Portugal's 1974 Carnation Revolution and the Lusaka Accord. First president Samora Machel (killed in a 1986 plane crash in South Africa).
  4. Civil war 1977–1992 between the FRELIMO government and RENAMO, which was backed first by Rhodesia, then by apartheid South Africa. About a million dead and millions displaced, including over 1.5 million refugees in neighbouring countries.
  5. Rome General Peace Accords, 4 October 1992, mediated by the Catholic Community of Sant'Egidio. The UN peacekeeping mission ONUMOZ (1992–94) oversaw demobilisation and the 1994 elections; it is often cited as a UN success.
  6. Renewed RENAMO fighting 2013–2019, ended by the Maputo Accord (6 August 2019); disarmament of RENAMO fighters completed in 2023. Presidents since 1975: Machel, Chissano, Guebuza, Nyusi (2015–2025), Chapo (2025–).

Foreign policy principles

  • Non-alignment and "friends with all": close historic ties with Russia and China (Soviet-era support for FRELIMO; Russian FM Lavrov visited Maputo in July 2026), while working closely with the EU, the US, Portugal and Rwanda. Mozambique abstained on the 2022 GA resolution condemning Russia's invasion of Ukraine.
  • African solutions and regional solidarity: a founding member of SADC (via SADCC, 1980) and a strong believer in regional security cooperation, shown by SAMIM.
  • Sovereignty first, but welcomes partners: Mozambique invites help on its terms (Rwanda, SADC, the EU) and resists outside conditions.
  • Peace through dialogue: it is proud of the 1992 and 2019 peace accords and of ONUMOZ, so it argues for political solutions, reconciliation and reintegration.
  • Development is security: the official line on Cabo Delgado pairs military action with jobs, services and the Northern Integrated Development Agency (ADIN, created 2020).

Groupings and UN record

  • UN regional group: African States. Also: African Union, SADC, the Group of 77 and China, Non-Aligned Movement, LDCs, Commonwealth (1995; the first member never ruled by Britain), the Community of Portuguese Language Countries (CPLP, founding member 1996), the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, Indian Ocean Rim Association.
  • Security Council: elected member 2023–2024, its first term ever. Priorities included counter-terrorism, women, peace and security, and African peace operations.
  • Hosted ONUMOZ (1992–94), one of the UN's peacekeeping success stories.
  • Disarmament credentials: declared free of known landmines in 2015 after decades of clearance; party to the Mine Ban Treaty, the Convention on Cluster Munitions (2011), the Chemical Weapons Convention (2000), the NPT and the African nuclear-weapon-free-zone Treaty of Pelindaba. Not a party to the CCW (the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, where autonomous weapons are negotiated).
  • Refugee law: 1951 Refugee Convention (acceded 1983, with reservations on several articles, including freedom of movement), the 1967 Protocol, the OAU Refugee Convention (1969), the Kampala Convention on internally displaced persons (ratified 2017), and its own Policy and Strategy for the Management of Internally Displaced Persons (2021).
  • Private military: not a participant in the Montreux Document and not a party to the 1989 UN Mercenaries Convention (per Mozambican civil society, 2020).

How to sound like Mozambique

Model answer: "Mozambique speaks from experience. History of the committee

  • 1945–46: created as the Political and Security Committee, handling all political and security items, including disarmament.
  • First GA resolution ever, 1(I) of January 1946, set up the UN Atomic Energy Commission to deal with atomic weapons: disarmament was on the agenda from day one.
  • 1978: after the first Special Session on Disarmament (SSOD-I), the First Committee was limited to disarmament and related international security questions. Other political items moved to the Special Political Committee (merged with the Fourth Committee in 1993).
  • Later special sessions: SSOD-II (1982) and SSOD-III (1988) ended without agreed outcomes. A fourth special session (SSOD-IV) has been discussed for decades but never convened.

Mandate

The First Committee deals with disarmament, global challenges and threats to peace that affect the international community, and seeks solutions to the challenges in the international security regime. It considers all disarmament and international security matters within the scope of the Charter or relating to the powers and functions of any other UN organ.

Charter basis:

  • Article 11(1): the GA "may consider the general principles of co-operation in the maintenance of international peace and security, including the principles governing disarmament and the regulation of armaments, and may make recommendations".
  • Article 11(2): it may discuss any question on peace and security brought by a member, the Security Council or a non-member.
  • Article 12: it may not make recommendations on a dispute the Security Council is currently dealing with (unless the Council asks).
  • Article 26: the Security Council is responsible for plans to regulate armaments. GA1 sets norms; the Council acts.
  • Article 13: the GA encourages the progressive development and codification of international law, which is why GA1 can launch treaty work.

Concerned with a certain group of people? No. It concerns all states, and increasingly human security (civilians, victims, survivors).

Functions and powers

  1. Debate and recommend: adopt resolutions setting principles and norms (nuclear disarmament, small arms, autonomous weapons, outer space, ICT security).
  2. Request reports: ask the Secretary-General to collect states' views and report (A/RES/78/241 on autonomous weapons led to report A/79/88 in 2024).
  3. Create bodies and processes: Groups of Governmental Experts (GGEs, a limited number of experts), Open-Ended Working Groups (OEWGs, open to all states), informal consultations, scientific panels, studies.
  4. Launch and adopt treaties: mandate negotiations and recommend finished treaties for adoption, for example the Arms Trade Treaty (process launched 2006, adopted by the GA 2013), the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (negotiated under a GA mandate, 2017), and the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (adopted by the GA 1996).
  5. Transparency tools: run the UN Register of Conventional Arms (1991) and the UN Report on Military Expenditures (1980), where states voluntarily report.
  6. Convene conferences and special sessions, including the conference on a Middle East zone free of nuclear and other weapons of mass destruction (held annually since 2019).

What it cannot do: bind states, impose sanctions or arms embargoes, authorise the use of force, deploy missions or inspect facilities. Those belong to the Security Council and to treaty bodies like the IAEA and OPCW.

How a session works

  1. General debate (about eight meetings): each state, and groups such as the African Group, NAM and the EU, sets out its priorities.
  2. Thematic discussion (about two weeks) in seven clusters:
    1. Nuclear weapons
    2. Other weapons of mass destruction (chemical, biological)
    3. Outer space (disarmament aspects)
    4. Conventional weapons (both your topics: private military companies and autonomous weapons, plus small arms, landmines, cluster munitions, ammunition)
    5. Other disarmament measures and international security (ICT, AI, gender, development)
    6. Regional disarmament and security
    7. Disarmament machinery
    Panels with the High Representative, heads of treaty bodies and experts take place in this phase.
  3. Action on all drafts (about a week): votes on resolutions and decisions, explanations of vote, and rights of reply.

Drafts are tabled by a main sponsor (often a state or group with a long-standing interest: Austria on autonomous weapons, Japan on nuclear disarmament, Mexico and Ireland on humanitarian disarmament, the NAM on many nuclear texts) with co-sponsors. Many resolutions are "biennialised" or "triennialised" (taken up every two or three years) to save time.

Voting and decisions

  • Each member has one vote.
  • In the committee, decisions are taken by a simple majority of members present and voting (abstentions do not count).
  • Many texts are adopted without a vote (by consensus), but the share is shrinking. Nakamitsu told the 81st session that "increasing numbers of votes and rights of reply" show a more divided committee.
  • States can request a recorded vote (each state's vote is listed), separate votes on individual paragraphs, and give explanations of vote.
  • In the plenary, Charter Article 18(2) lists "recommendations with respect to the maintenance of international peace and security" among "important questions" needing a two-thirds majority.
  • Any programme budget implications (costs of a new process) go to the Fifth Committee before plenary adoption.

Recent priorities and the 81st session (2026)

From the High Representative's opening speech (6 October 2026), the three main challenges:

  1. Nuclear risk "higher than at any time since the Cold War". The last US–Russia arms control treaty (New START) has expired, so for the first time in fifty years there are no caps on the two largest arsenals. Three NPT Review Conferences in a row have failed.
  2. Record military spending: $2.887 trillion in 2025, the highest ever, up 41% in ten years, while the SDGs fall behind.
  3. Military AI: AI and autonomy are spreading across armed forces. Informal exchanges on AI in the military domain, created by GA1, have begun, and Nakamitsu called for a dedicated multilateral forum. She noted agreed elements for an instrument on lethal autonomous weapons as a recent success.

Budget

  • GA1 has no budget of its own; its meetings are paid for by the UN regular budget.
  • UNODA is funded partly by the regular budget (assessed contributions) and heavily by voluntary contributions and trust funds, such as the UN Trust Facility Supporting Cooperation on Arms Regulation (UNSCAR) and the Saving Lives Entity (SALIENT) fund for small arms.
  • Any new meeting or expert group created by a resolution carries programme budget implications that the Fifth Committee must approve. The UN's liquidity crisis and the UN80 reform initiative (2025) are squeezing disarmament work too.

Controversies and criticisms

  • Polarisation: more recorded votes, rival resolutions (for example, competing texts on outer space from Russia and from Western states) and rights of reply.
  • "Talking shop" criticism: GA1 cannot bind anyone, and the CD it relies on for negotiations has been stuck since 1996.
  • Nuclear divide: TPNW supporters (most of the Global South, including Africa) versus nuclear-armed states and their allies, which reject it.
  • Treaty withdrawals: European states leaving the landmine and cluster munition treaties, criticised as reversing humanitarian progress.
  • Consensus as a veto: in the CCW and CD, one state can block everything, which is why many states push to move autonomous weapons talks to the GA.
  • Middle East WMD-free zone: Israel and the US do not take part in the annual conference.

Topic: private military and security companies

About 30 min

GA1 topic 1: "Strengthening International Frameworks on the Activities of Private Military and Security Companies". This is your strongest topic: Mozambique is one of very few states to have hired both Russian and Western-linked contractors in the same conflict.

Definitions

  • PMSCs (Montreux Document definition): "private business entities that provide military and/or security services, irrespective of how they describe themselves". Services include armed guarding and protection of people and objects, maintaining and operating weapons systems, detaining prisoners, and advising or training armed forces.
  • Mercenary (Additional Protocol I, Article 47, 1977): someone who meets six cumulative conditions: specially recruited to fight; takes a direct part in hostilities; motivated by private gain and paid much more than regular soldiers; not a national or resident of a party; not a member of a party's armed forces; not sent officially by another state. Because all six must be met, almost no contractor qualifies. This is the core legal gap.
  • Inherently state functions: tasks that should never be outsourced, above all direct participation in combat and detention. Where to draw this line is a central debate.
  • Do not confuse: a PMSC (a company), a mercenary (a legal status), and state-to-state deployments (Rwanda in Mozambique), which are lawful when the host consents.

Why it matters

  • Scale: in Iraq and Afghanistan, contractors at times outnumbered US troops. The industry is worth hundreds of billions of dollars a year.
  • Accountability gap: contractors fall between national jurisdictions and the law of armed conflict. Victims rarely get remedies.
  • Landmark abuses: Blackwater's Nisour Square shooting, Baghdad 2007 (17 Iraqi civilians killed); the Moura massacre, Mali, March 2022 (about 500 killed; the UN human rights office implicated Malian troops and "foreign military personnel", widely reported as Wagner).
  • Africa as the main theatre: Wagner in the Central African Republic (from 2018), Sudan, Libya (2019–20), Mozambique (2019) and Mali (from 2021). After Prigozhin's death in August 2023, Wagner's African operations were folded into Russia's state-run Africa Corps; Wagner left Mali in 2025 and Africa Corps stayed. Deals often trade security for mining concessions. Earlier: Executive Outcomes in Angola and Sierra Leone (1990s).
  • The UN itself uses private security companies to guard premises, under its 2012 guidelines.

Mozambique's own case (your best material)

  1. Wagner Group, from September 2019. About 200 Russian contractors with helicopters deployed to Cabo Delgado. They did not know the terrain, clashed with Mozambican troops, suffered deadly ambushes and pulled back within months.
  2. Dyck Advisory Group (DAG), April 2020 to April 2021. A South African company founded by former Zimbabwean colonel Lionel Dyck, flying helicopter gunships. Amnesty International's March 2021 report "What I Saw Is Death" (based on 79 interviews with displaced people) accused DAG of firing indiscriminately into crowds, alongside abuses by government forces and by the insurgents.
  3. Other private firms (Paramount, Burnham Global) supplied equipment and training.
  4. The shift to states: after the Palma attack, Mozambique turned to Rwanda (July 2021, bilateral) and SADC's SAMIM (July 2021 to July 2024), with EU training. They retook Mocímboa da Praia in August 2021.

The lesson Mozambique draws: private contractors failed militarily and left an accountability gap, while state and regional deployments under clear mandates worked better. Contractors are a symptom of weak state capacity; the real answer is capable, accountable national forces and regional cooperation.

Mozambique's position (how to present it)

Note: Mozambique has not published a detailed position on this topic. This is the policy that follows from its record and its alignment with the African Group. Present it confidently, but if pushed, say it is built on Mozambique's experience and the African Group's stance.

  • Supports a legally binding international framework, developed through the Human Rights Council's intergovernmental working group, with GA1 giving political backing.
  • Clear limits: no outsourcing of direct participation in hostilities or detention.
  • Home-state responsibility: countries where PMSCs are registered must license, vet and prosecute them, so host states like Mozambique are not left alone.
  • Remedies for victims, including in the country where the harm happened.
  • Capacity and security sector reform so states do not need contractors: training, funding (for example the AU–UN financing model in S/RES/2719 of 2023), and regional forces like SAMIM.
  • Respect for sovereignty: states may seek lawful security help, but rules must apply equally to all providers, Russian, Western or anyone else.

Possible resolution ideas

  • Call on the HRC working group to finalise a draft binding instrument by a set date.
  • Encourage states to join the Montreux Document and require ICoCA membership in all their contracts as an interim step (Mozambique could announce that it is considering joining).
  • A voluntary UN registry of PMSCs and contracts, run by UNODA or OHCHR.
  • Model national legislation and licensing, with UNREC training for African states.
  • Updating the 1977 OAU Convention through the African Union.

Tough questions to expect

  • "Mozambique hired Wagner. Why should anyone listen to you?" Because we learned the hard way. We have seen what happens without rules, and that is exactly why we want them. Own it; don't dodge.
  • "Mozambique hasn't joined the Montreux Document or the 1989 Convention." Acknowledge it: both have gaps (non-binding; outdated definition), which is why Mozambique wants a single modern framework, and joining Montreux is a reasonable step meanwhile.
  • "Is Rwanda's deployment any different from Wagner?" Yes: it is a state-to-state deployment at Mozambique's request, by a state's armed forces under its chain of command and accountable under international law. Contractors are accountable to a client and shareholders.
  • "What can GA1 do if the treaty is negotiated in the HRC?" Give political direction, ask for Secretary-General reports, connect the human rights and security tracks, and build the majority a treaty needs.

Topic: lethal autonomous weapon systems

About 28 min

GA1 topic 2: "Lethal Autonomous Weapon Systems and the Preservation of Meaningful Human Control". Mozambique has no special record here, so your strength must be knowing the process precisely and speaking for the African and Global South view.

Definitions

  • Lethal autonomous weapon systems (LAWS): there is no agreed definition. The ICRC's working definition: weapons that select and apply force to targets without human intervention. After a person activates them, they strike based on what their sensors detect matching a "target profile". The US definition (Directive 3000.09, updated 2023): once activated, can select and engage targets without further human intervention.
  • Automatic versus autonomous: landmines and defensive systems like the Phalanx gun or Iron Dome react automatically in narrow situations; the concern is systems that search for and choose targets across time and space, increasingly using AI.
  • Human in, on or out of the loop: a human decides each strike (in), supervises and can stop it (on), or is absent (out).
  • Meaningful human control: the idea that people must keep enough understanding, judgement and control over a weapon to be responsible for its effects. It has no agreed definition either, but is the heart of the topic.

Why it matters

  • International humanitarian law: can a machine apply distinction (civilians versus fighters), proportionality and precaution? The Martens Clause adds "principles of humanity and the dictates of public conscience".
  • Accountability gap: if a machine kills unlawfully, who is responsible: the commander, programmer or manufacturer?
  • Human dignity: a machine should not decide who lives or dies.
  • Proliferation: cheap drones and software spread to armed groups. Islamic State affiliates already use commercial drones.
  • Lower threshold for war and fast escalation when no soldiers are at risk.
  • Bias and error: algorithms trained on limited data may misidentify people, with darker-skinned people and the Global South most at risk.
  • Real-world use: a 2021 UN Panel of Experts report on Libya (S/2021/229) said Turkish-made Kargu-2 drones "hunted down" retreating forces in 2020, possibly without a human link. AI-assisted targeting has been reported in Ukraine, Gaza and, in 2026, Iran.

History in brief

  • League of Nations era: Fridtjof Nansen became the first High Commissioner for Refugees in 1921 (for Russian refugees) and created the Nansen passport (1922). The Nansen International Office for Refugees won the 1938 Nobel Peace Prize.
  • 1940s: UNRRA (1943) and the International Refugee Organization (1946–1952), which resettled over a million displaced Europeans.
  • 1950–51: UNHCR created with a three-year mandate and a tiny budget, mainly for Europe; the 1951 Convention adopted.
  • 1960s–70s: crises spread to Africa and Asia; the 1967 Protocol made refugee law universal; the 1969 OAU Convention widened the definition in Africa.
  • 1990s: UNHCR moves into war zones (Bosnia, Rwanda) and begins helping IDPs and returnees. 1992–96: about 1.7 million Mozambican refugees came home from Malawi and other neighbours after the Rome peace accords, then the largest repatriation UNHCR had organised in Africa.
  • 2003: GA removes the time limit on the mandate.
  • 2005: humanitarian reform and the cluster approach give UNHCR lead roles for IDPs.
  • 2016–18: New York Declaration (A/RES/71/1, 2016) and the Global Compact on Refugees (2018).
  • 2025–26: the worst funding crisis in its history; budget and staff cut sharply.

Mandate

UNHCR's mandate is to provide international protection to refugees and to seek permanent solutions to their problems (Statute, paragraph 1). Its work is "of an entirely non-political character" and "humanitarian and social" (paragraph 2).

  • Protection: making sure refugees can access asylum, are not returned to danger (non-refoulement), and enjoy their rights in the host country.
  • Durable solutions: voluntary repatriation in safety and dignity, local integration (including naturalisation) in the host country, or resettlement to a third country. Complementary pathways (study, work, family visas) supplement them.
  • Supervision: under Article 35 of the 1951 Convention (and Article II of the Protocol) states must cooperate with UNHCR, which supervises how they apply the Convention.
  • Expansion by the GA: Statute paragraph 9 lets the GA assign extra tasks. On that basis UNHCR now covers asylum seekers, returnees, stateless people (GA mandates of 1974 and 1995) and internally displaced persons (with the consent of the state).

Concerned with a certain group of people? Yes: forcibly displaced and stateless people. That is the key difference from IOM (all migrants) and from the human rights bodies (everyone). Palestine refugees served by UNRWA are excluded under Article 1D of the Convention.

Functions and powers

  1. Promote the conclusion, ratification and application of refugee conventions.
  2. Register refugees and decide refugee status where states cannot or do not (mandate refugee status determination).
  3. Advise governments on asylum law and policy; issue legal guidelines (Handbook on Procedures and Criteria for Determining Refugee Status, 1979, updated).
  4. Deliver emergency assistance: shelter, core relief items, water, cash, health and education support, often with partners.
  5. Run or support camps and settlements, and increasingly support refugees living in cities.
  6. Lead the global Protection Cluster and co-lead Shelter and Camp Coordination and Camp Management for IDPs.
  7. Negotiate voluntary repatriation agreements (often tripartite: country of origin, host country, UNHCR) and refer refugees for resettlement.
  8. Prevent and reduce statelessness.

Limits: UNHCR cannot force a state to admit refugees, cannot operate without the host government's consent, has no enforcement powers, and depends almost entirely on voluntary funding.

Recent priorities (77th ExCom, October 2026)

  • Delivering protection with far less money: prioritising life-saving aid, cutting headquarters costs, closing or merging offices.
  • Solutions and returns: supporting the record wave of returns while insisting they are voluntary, safe and dignified.
  • Burden- and responsibility-sharing under the Global Compact, ahead of the 2027 Global Refugee Forum.
  • Resolving protracted refugee situations (a support group of states made a joint statement at the 77th session).
  • Inclusion of refugees in national systems (health, education, jobs) rather than parallel services.

Controversies and criticisms

  • Funding collapse: donor cuts, led by the US, forced UNHCR to shed thousands of staff and close the Southern Africa bureau; critics warn protection is being sacrificed.
  • Resettlement collapse: the US, historically the largest resettlement country, suspended its refugee admissions programme in January 2025.
  • Premature returns: mass returns to Afghanistan (many forced from Iran and Pakistan) and to Syria after December 2024 raise doubts about whether returns are voluntary and safe.
  • Externalisation deals: UNHCR criticised the UK–Rwanda plan (scrapped 2024) and has raised concerns about offshore processing such as Italy–Albania.
  • Refoulement by states: pushbacks at Europe's borders and, in Africa, Tanzania's forced return of over 10,000 Mozambicans in 2021.
  • Encampment: long-term camps (Dadaab and Kakuma in Kenya) criticised for keeping people dependent; the Global Compact pushes self-reliance instead.
  • Accountability: a 2002 UNHCR and Save the Children report exposed sexual exploitation of refugee children by aid workers in West Africa, leading to strict UN rules on sexual exploitation and abuse.
  • IDP role: debate over whether UNHCR should do more for IDPs (the majority of the displaced) or stay focused on refugees.

Topic: psychosocial support for refugees and displaced persons

About 35 min

UNHCR topic 1: "Psychosocial Support for Refugees and Displaced Persons". The field is called MHPSS: mental health and psychosocial support.

Definitions

  • MHPSS (IASC, 2007): "any type of local or outside support that aims to protect or promote psychosocial well-being and/or prevent or treat mental disorder".
  • Psychosocial: the link between psychological effects (emotions, thoughts) and social factors (family, community, culture). Most distress after displacement is a normal reaction to abnormal events, not illness.
  • Mental disorder versus distress: only some people need clinical care; everyone benefits from safety, dignity and community.
  • Task-shifting: training non-specialists (nurses, community workers, teachers, refugees themselves) to deliver basic psychological help where specialists are scarce.

The IASC intervention pyramid

From the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Guidelines on MHPSS in Emergency Settings (2007), the key framework. Four layers, from everyone at the bottom to the few at the top:

  1. Basic services and security delivered in a dignified way (shelter, food, water, safety).
  2. Community and family supports (tracing families, child-friendly spaces, community activities, schooling).
  3. Focused, non-specialised supports (psychological first aid, basic counselling by trained non-specialists).
  4. Specialised services (psychiatrists, psychologists) for the small group with severe conditions.

Core principles: human rights and equity, participation, do no harm, build on existing resources, integrated services, multi-layered support.

The scale of the problem

  • A WHO-supported study in The Lancet (2019) estimated that about one in five people (22%) in conflict-affected settings has depression, anxiety, PTSD, bipolar disorder or schizophrenia.
  • Stress comes in three stages: before flight (violence, loss), during the journey (smuggling, detention, sexual violence) and after arrival (uncertainty, poverty, discrimination, no right to work).
  • Workforce gap: low-income countries have fewer than two mental health workers per 100,000 people (WHO Mental Health Atlas), against dozens in rich countries.
  • Children and adolescents, survivors of sexual and gender-based violence, former child soldiers, and people with disabilities are most at risk.

Main debates

  • Funding: MHPSS is usually among the first services cut. With UNHCR only about 35% funded in 2026, programmes are shrinking.
  • Clinical versus community approaches: Western trauma models (PTSD diagnosis, one-to-one therapy) versus community-based, culturally grounded support.
  • Integration: stand-alone projects versus building MHPSS into health, education and protection services, and into national health systems that host communities also use.
  • Digital tools: apps and tele-counselling can reach more people but raise privacy and access concerns.
  • Self-reliance: the right to work and study is itself one of the strongest protections for mental health.

How UNHCR actually does MHPSS

  • Integrated, not stand-alone: UNHCR's 2013 Operational Guidance says MHPSS belongs in two sectors at once: health (identifying and treating mental conditions in primary care) and community-based protection (rebuilding social support, preventing violence, helping people with disabilities and survivors).
  • Community-based protection: refugee outreach volunteers, women's and youth groups, and refugee-led organisations identify people in distress and refer them.
  • Psychological First Aid for all front-line staff, including registration and border staff.
  • mhGAP training so general doctors and nurses in refugee clinics can treat depression, psychosis, epilepsy and substance use.
  • Scalable psychological interventions from WHO (Problem Management Plus, Self-Help Plus, Step-by-Step digital self-help), tested in randomised trials with Syrian refugees and with South Sudanese refugees in Uganda.
  • Monitoring through UNHCR's health information system, which records mental health consultations in camps.
  • Staff care: humanitarian workers themselves face burnout and trauma.

Case studies

  • Rohingya in Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh (about a million people): large community-based MHPSS programmes run by UN agencies and NGOs, with Rohingya volunteers as psychosocial workers; cuts in food aid have increased distress.

Mozambique's angle

  • Cabo Delgado's trauma: beheadings, burned villages, abductions of children (some forced to fight), sexual violence, and people displaced several times, then hit by cyclones. About 80% of the newly displaced are women and children.
  • Specialists are very scarce in Mozambique, so it relies on community-based care and task-shifting, such as mid-level psychiatric technicians and trained community workers.
  • Experience after the civil war: communities reintegrated former child soldiers partly through traditional cleansing and reconciliation ceremonies, combined with family reunification. This is a well-known example of culturally grounded psychosocial recovery.
  • Host communities in Cabo Delgado and Nampula are as poor as the displaced, so services must serve both.

Mozambique's position (how to present it)

  • MHPSS is life-saving, not optional, and should get a protected share of humanitarian appeals.
  • Community-based and culturally grounded approaches, following the IASC pyramid; train teachers, health workers, religious leaders and displaced people themselves.
  • Integrate MHPSS into national primary health care, so it serves refugees, IDPs and host communities and lasts after the emergency.
  • Special focus on children and survivors of sexual violence: child-friendly spaces, reintegration of abducted children, safe schools.
  • Fair burden-sharing: host and conflict-affected countries carry the load; donors must fund it predictably, including through pledges at the 2027 Global Refugee Forum.
  • Apply it equally to IDPs, who are the majority of the displaced.

Possible resolution ideas

  • A minimum MHPSS funding share in every UNHCR appeal.
  • Scale up WHO and UNHCR non-specialist programmes through training of community workers.
  • MHPSS pledges at the 2027 Global Refugee Forum.
  • Better data: include mental health indicators in UNHCR needs assessments.
  • Protect refugees' right to work and study as a mental health measure.

Tough questions to expect

  • "With UNHCR only a third funded, why should money go to mental health instead of food and shelter?" Because they are not alternatives: the IASC pyramid starts with basic services delivered with dignity, and community-based MHPSS is cheap. Untreated trauma undermines everything else, from children's learning to people's ability to work and return home.
  • "Aren't Western trauma models unsuitable in Africa?" That is exactly Mozambique's point: support must be community-based and culturally grounded, as in our reintegration of child soldiers after the civil war, while still offering clinical care to the few who need it.
  • "Does Mozambique have the capacity to deliver this?" Not alone. It has very few specialists, which is why it relies on task-shifting and why international cooperation and predictable funding matter.
  • "What can UNHCR do that WHO can't?" UNHCR is in the camps, settlements and border points; it can build MHPSS into protection, registration and community work, while WHO sets clinical standards.

Topic: protection along migration routes

About 28 min

UNHCR topic 2: "Strengthening Protection for Refugees and Asylum Seekers along Migration Routes". The focus is on people on the move: before they reach a country that can grant asylum.

Definitions

  • Mixed movements: refugees, asylum seekers and migrants travelling together, on the same routes, using the same smugglers, facing the same dangers, but with different legal rights.
  • Refugee versus migrant: a refugee is protected by the 1951 Convention and must not be returned to danger; a migrant moves for other reasons but still has human rights. "Asylum seeker" means someone whose claim has not yet been decided.
  • Smuggling versus trafficking (the 2000 Palermo Protocols to the UN Convention against Transnational Organized Crime): smuggling is a paid service to cross a border illegally, a crime against the state, and it ends at the border; trafficking is exploitation through force, fraud or coercion, a crime against the person, and it can happen without crossing a border.
  • Non-refoulement: the ban on returning anyone to persecution or serious harm (1951 Convention Art. 33; Convention against Torture Art. 3). It is customary international law, so it binds even states that have not ratified the Refugee Convention, and it applies at the border, not only inside the country.
  • Pushback: forcing people back across a border without assessing their claims; a breach of non-refoulement.
  • Externalisation: rich states paying or partnering with transit states to stop people before they arrive.
  • Route-based approach: UNHCR's strategy (with IOM) of offering protection, information and alternatives all along a route, in countries of origin, transit and destination, instead of only at the end.

Legal and policy framework

  • 1951 Convention: non-refoulement (Art. 33), no penalties for illegal entry (Art. 31).
  • Law of the sea: the duty to rescue people in distress at sea (UNCLOS Art. 98, the SOLAS and SAR conventions).
  • Palermo Protocols (2000) on smuggling and trafficking.
  • OAU Refugee Convention (1969) and the Kampala Convention (2009) for Africa.
  • Global Compact on Refugees and Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration (both 2018). The migration compact's Objective 8 commits states to save lives and look for missing migrants.
  • Regional policies: the EU Pact on Migration and Asylum (2024, applying from June 2026); externalisation deals such as EU–Türkiye (2016), Italy–Libya (2017), EU–Tunisia (2023) and Italy–Albania (2023). The UK–Rwanda plan was scrapped in 2024.

Main debates

  • Border control versus access to asylum: states' right to control borders against the duty not to push people back.
  • Externalisation: does it save lives or just move the danger to Libya or the Sahara?
  • Search and rescue: state-run rescue versus criminalising NGO rescue ships.
  • Safe and legal pathways (resettlement, humanitarian visas, family reunification, labour mobility) as the real alternative to smugglers.
  • Burden-sharing: transit states in the Global South carry the risk while funding falls.

Mozambique on the southern route

  • Migrants from Ethiopia and Somalia, and refugees from the Great Lakes, travel south through Tanzania and Malawi, cross into Mozambique (often in Tete or Niassa) and head for South Africa. IOM recorded about 33,700 movements from Mozambique to South Africa in 2025, mostly Mozambicans and Malawians.
  • The Tete tragedy, 24 March 2020: police stopped a truck coming from Malawi and found 64 Ethiopian men dead from suffocation in a sealed container; 14 survived. The Mozambican driver admitted he had been paid to smuggle them. Similar finds followed in the region: a mass grave of Ethiopian migrants in northern Malawi (2022) and bodies dumped near Lusaka, Zambia (2022).
  • Mozambicans as asylum seekers: people fleeing Cabo Delgado tried to cross into Tanzania, which forcibly returned over 10,000 of them in 2021 through the Negomano border post. UNHCR called this refoulement. Mozambique knows from its own citizens why non-refoulement matters.
  • Returns from South Africa: Mozambicans deported from South Africa, and xenophobic violence there, are a running concern.
  • Mozambique has a 2008 law against trafficking in persons and works with IOM and SADC on migration.

Mozambique's position (how to present it)

  • Non-refoulement is absolute, at borders and at sea; Mozambique speaks from the 2021 experience of its own citizens being pushed back.
  • Protect people, prosecute smugglers: target smuggling and trafficking networks, never the people they move; identify trafficking victims and refugees early.
  • Regional cooperation through SADC and the AU: shared data, joint training for border officials, the Southern African migration dialogue (MIDSA), cooperation with IOM and UNHCR.
  • Protection-sensitive border management: screening and referral to asylum at border posts, not only security checks.
  • Safe and legal pathways and labour mobility in southern Africa as alternatives to smugglers.
  • Fair burden-sharing: transit and host states in Africa need predictable funding; the closure of UNHCR's Southern Africa bureau shows the cost of donor cuts.
  • Applies the African definitions: the OAU Convention's wider refugee definition and the Kampala Convention.

Possible resolution ideas

  • Expand UNHCR's route-based approach to the southern route, with protection points in Malawi, Mozambique and Zimbabwe.
  • Regional SADC protocol on search for missing migrants and identification of the dead.
  • Training and referral systems at border posts, funded through the Global Compact.
  • More resettlement and complementary pathways from transit countries.
  • Independent monitoring of pushbacks.

Tough questions to expect

  • "Mozambique has reservations to the Refugee Convention, including on freedom of movement. Isn't that hypocritical?" In practice Mozambique applies them flexibly and many refugees live in cities; it is reviewing its framework with UNHCR, and it adopted an IDP policy and ratified the Kampala Convention. Concede, then pivot.
  • "Should states be allowed to stop migrants at borders?" States can manage borders, but every person must be able to ask for asylum and must not be pushed back to danger.
  • "What should UNHCR do about smuggling? It isn't a police agency." UNHCR identifies and protects refugees within mixed flows, gives information about risks, and advocates for pathways; law enforcement belongs to UNODC and states.

How NMUN-NY works (know this cold)

  • The conference: National Model United Nations, New York, every spring, with thousands of university students. Committees meet over several days, partly in UN Headquarters. Your university's delegation represents one country (Mozambique) across all its committees.
  • The Dais: each committee is run by a Director and Assistant Director (NMUN Secretariat staff), sometimes with a student Chair. The Dais can rule out motions that are dilatory or disruptive.
  • Before: the Background Guide for each committee, and a position paper per committee (your country's policy on both topics). Position papers can win awards.
  • No pre-written resolutions. NMUN does not accept them: any clause produced by one person, or with AI, without being discussed and negotiated in a working group counts as pre-written. You can bring ideas, not finished clauses.
  • Philosophy: NMUN uses the real rules of procedure of each UN body as closely as possible and stresses the sovereign equality of states. Diplomacy and consensus are rewarded, not domination.

Law, rights and security

International law essentials

About 20 min

International law is the body of rules that binds states and international organizations in their relations with each other. Public international law governs states; private international law deals with cross-border disputes between private parties (which country's law applies).

Sources: Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute

  1. Treaties (conventions): bind only states that have consented.
  2. Customary international law: general state practice accepted as law (opinio juris). Binds all states, except a persistent objector that objected clearly and consistently while the rule was forming.
  3. General principles of law recognized by nations: good faith, estoppel, pacta sunt servanda (treaties must be kept). Used to fill gaps.
  4. Judicial decisions and the writings of leading scholars: "subsidiary means" for identifying the law.

GA resolutions are not on the list, but can be evidence of custom.

Jus cogens (peremptory norms)

Rules from which no derogation is permitted; a treaty conflicting with one is void (VCLT Art. 53). The persistent objector rule does not apply to them. Commonly accepted examples (International Law Commission, 2022): prohibition of aggression, genocide, crimes against humanity, slavery, torture, racial discrimination and apartheid, the basic rules of international humanitarian law, and the right of self-determination. Related idea: obligations erga omnes, owed to the whole international community, so any state can invoke them (this is why South Africa could sue Israel and The Gambia could sue Myanmar).

Note: Do not mix up jus cogens with the R2P list. R2P covers four crimes (genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity). Jus cogens is a broader category of norms.

Sovereignty and statehood

  • Westphalian sovereignty (Peace of Westphalia, 1648): each state has supreme authority over its territory and is equal in law; no interference in internal affairs. Reflected in Charter Arts. 2(1) and 2(7).
  • Internal sovereignty is control at home; external sovereignty is independence and recognition abroad.
  • Montevideo Convention (1933, Art. 1): a state has (a) a permanent population, (b) a defined territory, (c) a government, (d) the capacity to enter into relations with other states. Recognition is not one of the four criteria.
  • Declaratory theory: a state exists once it meets the criteria. Constitutive theory: a state exists only once others recognize it. Practice mixes both.
  • Self-determination (Charter Art. 1(2); GA resolution 1514 (XV), 1960, the Decolonization Declaration). The UN still lists 17 Non-Self-Governing Territories, including Western Sahara.
  • Uti possidetis juris: new states inherit colonial borders. A pillar of African Union policy.
  • Diplomatic relations are governed by the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961).

Custom in more depth

  • Examples of customary rules: diplomatic immunity (before it was codified in 1961), the prohibition of piracy, non-refoulement, the prohibition of torture, much of the UDHR and of IHL.
  • State practice must be widespread, representative and consistent; opinio juris is the belief it is legally required, not just habit or courtesy.
  • Evidence includes official statements, national laws and court decisions, votes on GA resolutions, and treaty ratification patterns. Judges identify custom; it is not written in one place, which makes it hard to prove.
  • Persistent objector: a state that objected clearly and consistently while the rule was forming is not bound (Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries, ICJ 1951). Rarely successful, and never against jus cogens.
  • Regional custom: possible between a group of states, but must be strictly proven (Asylum case, 1950: Colombia failed to prove a Latin American custom of diplomatic asylum).

General principles

Principles found across national legal systems "recognized by civilized nations" (old wording now read as "recognized by the community of nations"): good faith, pacta sunt servanda, estoppel, res judicata, the duty to repair harm caused. They fill gaps and help interpret treaties. Many carry Latin names because they come from Roman law.

Treaties: more vocabulary

  • Depositary: the body that holds the treaty and receives ratifications; for most multilateral treaties, the UN Secretary-General.
  • Interpretation (VCLT Arts. 31–32): ordinary meaning, in context, in light of object and purpose; preparatory work only as a supplement.
  • Derogation: temporarily suspending some obligations in an emergency (ICCPR Art. 4), never for the right to life, ban on torture or slavery.
  • Termination and withdrawal: as the treaty allows; otherwise only under VCLT rules (material breach, fundamental change of circumstances).
  • Treaty vs convention: legally identical. In practice "convention" is used for broad multilateral treaties open to all on a general subject (UNFCCC, CEDAW), and "treaty" for more specific arrangements, often among particular parties (peace treaties, border treaties), though usage is loose (NPT is a "treaty" open to all).
  • Covenant: a solemn treaty, mostly used for the two 1966 human rights covenants and the League Covenant.
  • Charter: the constituent treaty of an organization (UN Charter, African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights).

The Montevideo Convention in detail

  • Convention on the Rights and Duties of States, signed 26 December 1933 at the Seventh International Conference of American States in Montevideo, Uruguay; in force 26 December 1934. A regional treaty, but Article 1 is accepted as the customary definition of a state.
  • Article 1: permanent population, defined territory, government, capacity to enter into relations with other states.
  • Article 3: a state's political existence is independent of recognition (the declaratory theory).
  • Article 8: no state has the right to intervene in the internal or external affairs of another.
  • Article 11: no recognition of territory acquired by force.
  • The US (on intervention), Brazil and Peru signed with reservations.

Sovereignty in more depth

  • Peace of Westphalia (1648): ended the Thirty Years' War and the Eighty Years' War; the origin of the idea of independent, equal territorial states with no outside authority over religion or internal affairs.
  • Elements: supreme authority within the territory; independence from outside control; legal equality with other states; immunity of the state and its property from other states' courts.
  • Internal vs external sovereignty: internal is effective control and law-making at home; external is independence and recognition in international relations.
  • Sovereign immunity protects states from being sued in other states' courts without consent. The ICJ held in Germany v. Italy (2012) that immunity still applies even when serious violations (jus cogens) are alleged, so "violating jus cogens waives immunity" is not the current law, though it is a live debate, especially for individual officials before international courts.
  • Sovereignty as responsibility (R2P) and human rights law have limited what states can do to their own people.

Self-determination vs territorial integrity

Both are Charter principles. In the colonial context, self-determination meant independence (GA 1514 (XV), 1960; 1541 (XV) on the three options of independence, free association or integration). Outside colonialism, international law generally favours internal self-determination (autonomy, participation) over secession; "remedial secession" for oppressed peoples is debated. The Friendly Relations Declaration (A/RES/2625 (XXV), 1970) states both principles and is treated as reflecting custom.

Use of force and the laws of war

About 13 min

Jus ad bellum: when force is lawful

Article 2(4) bans the threat or use of force. There are only two clear exceptions:

  1. Self-defence (Art. 51) against an armed attack, individually or collectively, until the SC acts. It must be necessary and proportionate (the Caroline test) and reported to the SC.
  2. Security Council authorization under Chapter VII (Art. 42), usually phrased as "all necessary means" (Iraq/Kuwait 1990, S/RES/678; Libya 2011, S/RES/1973).

Contested: anticipatory or pre-emptive self-defence, humanitarian intervention without SC approval (NATO in Kosovo, 1999), self-defence against non-state actors, and intervention by invitation of a government.

Aggression is defined in GA resolution 3314 (XXIX) (1974) and is a crime under the Rome Statute (amendments activated 2018).

Jus in bello: international humanitarian law (IHL)

Rules that apply during armed conflict to all sides equally, whoever started it. Their purpose is to limit suffering.

  • Four Geneva Conventions (1949): I wounded and sick soldiers on land; II at sea; III prisoners of war; IV civilians. Universally ratified.
  • Additional Protocols: I (1977, international conflicts), II (1977, non-international conflicts), III (2005, the Red Crystal emblem).
  • Hague Conventions (1899, 1907): means and methods of warfare.
  • Core principles: distinction (civilians and civilian objects are never targets), proportionality (expected civilian harm must not be excessive compared with the military advantage), precaution, military necessity and humanity.
  • The ICRC (International Committee of the Red Cross) is the guardian of IHL.
  • Serious violations are war crimes, prosecutable by national courts (including under universal jurisdiction) and the ICC.

Humanitarian principles

Humanity, neutrality, impartiality, independence (A/RES/46/182, 1991, which also created the humanitarian coordination system now run by OCHA).

In depth

The just war tradition behind jus ad bellum

Older criteria for a lawful war: just cause, right (legitimate) authority, right intention, last resort, proportionality, and reasonable chance of success. The Charter replaced most of this with a single rule (Art. 2(4)) and two exceptions, but necessity and proportionality survive as conditions on self-defence.

Self-defence debates

  • The Caroline (1837): British forces destroyed a US steamer supplying Canadian rebels; US Secretary of State Webster set the test of a necessity "instant, overwhelming, leaving no choice of means, and no moment for deliberation".
  • Anticipatory self-defence against an imminent attack is widely accepted; pre-emptive self-defence against a non-imminent threat (the 2003 Iraq war rationale) is not.
  • Non-state actors: since 9/11 (S/RES/1368, 1373) several states claim self-defence against groups in states "unable or unwilling" to stop them (e.g. against ISIL in Syria); contested.
  • Collective self-defence: helping a victim state at its request (basis of NATO's Article 5).
  • Intervention by invitation: force at the request of the recognized government; disputed when the government's legitimacy is in doubt.
  • Humanitarian intervention without SC approval (Kosovo 1999) remains unlawful in most views; R2P channels it through the SC.

SC authorizations of force

Korea (1950, possible because the USSR was boycotting), Iraq/Kuwait (S/RES/678, 1990), Somalia (1992), Haiti (1994), Bosnia (NATO air power, 1990s), Libya (S/RES/1973, 2011), and robust peacekeeping mandates such as the DR Congo Force Intervention Brigade (2013).

IHL in more depth

  • Combatants may be attacked and, if captured, are prisoners of war (Geneva Convention III); they cannot be prosecuted merely for fighting. Civilians are protected unless and for such time as they take a direct part in hostilities.
  • International vs non-international armed conflicts: the full Conventions and Protocol I apply between states; civil wars are governed by Common Article 3 (minimum humane treatment) and Protocol II.
  • Occupation law: the occupier must maintain order and civilian life, may not transfer its own population into the territory (GC IV Art. 49) and must allow relief.
  • Protected persons and objects: the wounded and sick, medical staff and hospitals, humanitarian workers, cultural property (Hague Convention 1954), objects indispensable to survival (food, water).
  • Weapons law: bans on chemical and biological weapons, anti-personnel mines (Ottawa 1997), cluster munitions (Oslo 2008), blinding lasers; the CCW framework; weapons reviews for new technology (Additional Protocol I Art. 36), which is where the debate on autonomous weapons sits.
  • Enforcement: national courts, universal jurisdiction (states can prosecute grave breaches wherever committed), ad hoc tribunals, and the ICC.

Ethnic cleansing is not a separate legal crime: it is a political term for forced removal of a group, usually prosecuted as crimes against humanity or war crimes, which is why R2P lists it separately.

The Human Rights Council

  • Created by A/RES/60/251 on 15 March 2006 as a subsidiary organ of the GA, replacing the discredited Commission on Human Rights (an ECOSOC commission, 1946–2006), which was criticized for politicization and for abusive states sitting on it. First session June 2006, in Geneva.
  • 47 members, elected by the GA by absolute majority (97 votes) in a secret ballot, for three-year terms; no more than two consecutive terms. Candidates' human rights records are supposed to be taken into account.
  • Seats: Africa 13, Asia-Pacific 13, Latin America and Caribbean 8, Western Europe and Others 7, Eastern Europe 6.
  • Suspension by a two-thirds GA vote for gross and systematic violations: Libya (2011), Russia (April 2022).
  • At least three regular sessions a year (roughly February–April, June–July, September–October), at least ten weeks in total. A special session can be called by one-third of members.
  • The President and four Vice-Presidents form the Bureau, one per regional group, for a one-year cycle. 2026 President: Ambassador Sidharto Reza Suryodipuro (Indonesia).
  • Resolutions are not binding; its power is scrutiny, investigation, naming and standard-setting.
  • The United States withdrew from the Council again in 2025.

The HRC is a 47-member specialist body in Geneva that meets most of the year and runs investigative mechanisms. The Third Committee includes all 193 members in New York, meets in the autumn, adopts norm-setting resolutions and reviews the HRC's annual report and Special Procedures' reports to the GA.

UDHR and the covenants: more detail

  • The UDHR was drafted by a committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, with René Cassin (France), Charles Malik (Lebanon), P.C. Chang (China) and others. Adopted in Paris on 10 December 1948 by 48 votes, 0 against, 8 abstentions (the Soviet bloc, South Africa and Saudi Arabia).
  • Structure: preamble and 30 articles; Arts. 3–21 civil and political rights, Arts. 22–27 economic, social and cultural rights. Art. 18 freedom of religion; Art. 19 freedom of opinion and expression.
  • ICESCR: preamble and 31 articles in five parts; the Optional Protocol (2008) allows individual complaints. ICCPR: preamble and 53 articles in six parts.
  • "Three generations" of rights: civil and political; economic, social and cultural; collective rights (development, a healthy environment, peace). The 1993 Vienna Declaration rejected ranking them: all are "universal, indivisible, interdependent and interrelated".

The HRC year and leadership

  • Each year is a "cycle" (January–December) with three regular sessions: roughly February–April (the longest, about six weeks), June–July and September–October. Sessions are numbered consecutively (the 60th was in September–October 2025).
  • Recent Presidents: 2024 Omar Zniber (Morocco); 2025 Jürg Lauber (Switzerland); 2026 Sidharto Reza Suryodipuro (Indonesia). The first President (2006) was Luis Alfonso de Alba (Mexico).
  • The Bureau is the President and four Vice-Presidents, one per regional group; one Vice-President also serves as Rapporteur. It handles procedural and organizational matters.
  • Funding: the UN regular budget plus voluntary contributions to OHCHR (the annual UN Human Rights Appeal); chronic underfunding is a recurring problem.

The UPR step by step

  1. Three documents: the state's national report; a UN compilation (from treaty bodies and Special Procedures); a stakeholder summary (NGOs, national human rights institutions).
  2. Review in the UPR Working Group (all 47 HRC members; any UN member may speak), three and a half hours per state, with a "troika" of three rapporteur states drawn by lot.
  3. Other states make recommendations; the state under review "supports" or "notes" each.
  4. The HRC adopts the outcome at its next session; implementation is checked at the next review.

Pace: three Working Group sessions a year, 14 states each, 42 a year, all 193 in a 4.5-year cycle (HRC resolution 16/21). The fourth cycle started in November 2022.

Special Procedures in practice

  • About 46 thematic and 14 country mandates. Examples of thematic mandates: freedom of expression, human rights defenders, violence against women, torture, the right to a healthy environment, the rights of persons with disabilities, extreme poverty, contemporary racism, sale and sexual exploitation of children, rights of Indigenous Peoples. Country mandates include Myanmar, the DPRK, Iran, Afghanistan, Belarus, Syria and the Occupied Palestinian Territories.
  • Mandate holders are appointed by the HRC on the President's proposal, serve unpaid in their personal capacity, conduct country visits (only with the state's invitation), send communications to governments, and report annually to the HRC and often the GA Third Committee.
  • Controversy example: in July 2025 the United States imposed sanctions on Francesca Albanese, Special Rapporteur on the Occupied Palestinian Territories, after her reports accused Israel of genocide and named companies; many states and UN experts condemned the move.

Advisory Committee and Complaint Procedure details

  • Advisory Committee: 18 independent experts nominated by governments and elected by the HRC (normally at its September session), three-year terms renewable once; meets twice a year (one week in February, one in August); first met in August 2008; produces studies only when the HRC requests them; cannot adopt resolutions.
  • Complaint Procedure: confidential, for consistent patterns of gross violations. (1) Initial screening by the Chair of the Working Group on Communications with the Secretariat: in an official UN language, not manifestly politically motivated or abusive, domestic remedies tried. (2) The Working Group on Communications (meets twice a year) dismisses, asks for more information or passes it on. (3) The Working Group on Situations (twice a year) reports to the HRC on patterns. (4) The HRC, usually in private, can end the review, keep it under review, appoint an expert, make it public, or recommend OHCHR technical assistance.

Other HRC mechanisms

  • Commissions of inquiry and fact-finding missions (Syria since 2011, Myanmar, Ukraine, Sudan, Venezuela, the Occupied Palestinian Territory and Israel since 2021) gather evidence for accountability.
  • Open-ended intergovernmental working groups negotiate new instruments (e.g. on business and human rights, private military and security companies, the right to development). "Open-ended" means any UN member can join; "intergovernmental" means negotiators are states, with NGOs as observers.
  • Expert mechanisms (e.g. the Expert Mechanism on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples) and forums (Social Forum, Forum on Minority Issues, Forum on Business and Human Rights) meet annually.

Responsibility to Protect (R2P)

About 10 min

R2P is the political commitment that sovereignty is a responsibility, not just a right: states must protect their populations from four atrocity crimes, and if they manifestly fail, the international community should act.

Origins

  • The failures in Rwanda (1994: about 800,000 people, mostly Tutsi, killed in about 100 days while a small UN mission was cut back) and Srebrenica (1995: about 8,000 Bosniak men and boys killed in a declared UN "safe area").
  • Kofi Annan's challenge to reconcile sovereignty and intervention; the International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (ICISS), sponsored by Canada, coined the term in its 2001 report.
  • Endorsed unanimously at the 2005 World Summit, paragraphs 138–139 of the World Summit Outcome (A/RES/60/1).

Four crimes

Genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity. Nothing else (not natural disasters, not general human rights abuses).

Three pillars (Ban Ki-moon's 2009 report)

  1. The state's own responsibility to protect its population.
  2. The international community's responsibility to assist states in doing so (capacity-building, early warning).
  3. When a state is manifestly failing, the international community must take timely and decisive action through the UN Charter: peaceful means first, and force only through the Security Council.

Status and controversy

  • A political commitment, not a binding treaty. The underlying crimes are prohibited by law; R2P is about responding to them.
  • First major use: S/RES/1973 (2011, Libya), authorizing force to protect civilians. NATO's campaign ended in regime change, which made Russia, China and many Global South states suspicious of pillar three. Brazil proposed "Responsibility while Protecting" (2011) in response.
  • Syria showed the limits: repeated vetoes blocked action.
  • Veto restraint initiatives: the France–Mexico initiative (2015) asks the P5 voluntarily not to veto in mass-atrocity situations; the ACT Group's Code of Conduct (2015) asks all SC members not to vote against credible action to prevent atrocities, with 120+ supporters.
  • There is a UN Special Adviser on R2P, working with the Special Adviser on the Prevention of Genocide.

Model answer: If asked: "R2P reframes sovereignty as responsibility. It is strongest on pillars one and two, prevention and assistance, which most states support; pillar three remains contested after Libya, which is why many delegations prefer prevention and early warning."

Peacekeeping, peacebuilding and the PBC

About 18 min

Peacekeeping basics

  • Not mentioned in the Charter. It sits between Chapter VI and VII, sometimes called "Chapter VI and a half" (Hammarskjöld).
  • First missions: UNTSO (1948, Middle East, unarmed observers) and UNMOGIP (1949, India–Pakistan). Both are still running.
  • UNEF I (1956, Suez Crisis): the first armed peacekeeping force, devised by Lester Pearson and Dag Hammarskjöld.
  • ONUC (1960–64, Congo): the first large mission (about 20,000 troops).
  • Nobel Peace Prize 1988. Over 70 operations since 1948; more than 4,000 peacekeepers have died in service.
  • Three principles: consent of the parties, impartiality, non-use of force except in self-defence and defence of the mandate.
  • The UN has no army. States volunteer troops and police (largest contributors: Nepal, Bangladesh, India, Rwanda, Pakistan; Egypt is among the top contributors). Peacekeepers wear the blue helmet or beret to show UN neutrality.
  • Funded by a separate assessed budget; the largest payers are the US and China. The budget and number of missions have shrunk since 2023. MINUSMA (Mali) closed in 2023; the SC decided UNIFIL (Lebanon) will end after a final extension to 31 December 2026.

From peacekeeping to peacebuilding

Boutros-Ghali's An Agenda for Peace (1992) defined the toolbox: preventive diplomacy (before conflict), peacemaking (negotiating an end), peacekeeping (holding a ceasefire) and post-conflict peacebuilding (preventing relapse by building institutions, the economy and reconciliation). About half of countries emerging from conflict used to relapse within a decade.

The Peacebuilding Commission (PBC)

  • Created in 2005 by concurrent resolutions of the GA and SC (A/RES/60/180 and S/RES/1645) following the 2005 World Summit. An intergovernmental advisory body of both organs; it reports to both.
  • 31 members: 7 elected by the GA, 7 by the SC (including the P5), 7 by ECOSOC, the 5 largest financial contributors and the 5 largest troop and police contributors. Two-year terms.
  • Works by consensus; does not adopt binding decisions. It advises the SC, convenes actors (international financial institutions, regional organizations, civil society), focuses attention and keeps donors engaged.
  • Has worked on Burundi, Sierra Leone, Guinea-Bissau, the Central African Republic, Liberia, Guinea and regional situations (the Sahel, the Great Lakes, West Africa).
  • Supported by the Peacebuilding Support Office (PBSO) in DPPA and linked to the Peacebuilding Fund (PBF) (2006), the SG's fund for fast, catalytic financing. Since 2024, some assessed contributions go to the PBF.
  • Sustaining peace twin resolutions (2016: A/RES/70/262 and S/RES/2282) made prevention and peacebuilding a task for the whole UN system throughout the conflict cycle.
  • The peacebuilding architecture is reviewed every five years; the latest review concluded in 2025.

Transitions (NMUN 2027 PBC topic 1)

When a mission leaves (UNAMID in Darfur in 2020, MINUSMA in 2023, MONUSCO drawing down), gains can collapse. S/RES/2594 (2021) asks for early, integrated transition planning with national ownership, protection of civilians and continued financing. The PBC's role is to keep attention and money on the country after the peacekeepers leave.

Related agendas

Women, Peace and Security (S/RES/1325); Youth, Peace and Security (S/RES/2250); SDG 16 (peace, justice and strong institutions); A New Agenda for Peace (2023); the Pact for the Future (2024).

How a mission is created and run

  1. The SC adopts a resolution with a mandate (tasks, size, duration, usually renewed every year).
  2. The Secretariat (DPO with DOS) plans and asks member states for troops and police; the GA Fifth Committee approves the budget.
  3. A Special Representative of the Secretary-General heads the mission; a Force Commander leads the military component.
  4. Troop-contributing countries keep command over discipline and criminal jurisdiction over their soldiers; the UN reimburses them per soldier.

Facts that come up

  • Blue helmets and berets signal neutrality and UN authority; soldiers keep their national uniforms and weapons, supplied by their governments.
  • Uniformed personnel are nominated by their governments (you can't apply individually); civilians are recruited directly by the UN or through UN Volunteers.
  • More than 4,000 peacekeepers have died since 1948; the deadliest missions include ONUC, UNIFIL and MINUSMA. Attacks on peacekeepers can be war crimes.
  • Nobel Peace Prize 1988 for UN peacekeeping; Lester Pearson won in 1957 for UNEF.
  • Largest troop contributors are South Asian and African states (Nepal, Bangladesh, India, Rwanda, Pakistan, Ghana); Egypt is consistently among the top contributors. The largest funders of the peacekeeping budget are the US and China.
  • Women are still under 10% of military peacekeepers; the UN has set gender targets under its Uniformed Gender Parity Strategy.
  • Scandals: cholera introduced to Haiti by peacekeepers (2010, about 10,000 deaths; the UN apologized in 2016); sexual exploitation and abuse in several missions, leading to a zero-tolerance policy.

The PBC in more depth

  • It works through country configurations (Burundi, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea-Bissau, Central African Republic, Guinea) chaired by a member state, and increasingly through flexible meetings on regions (Sahel, Great Lakes, West Africa, Colombia, the Gambia) at a country's request.
  • Its added value: convening (governments, international financial institutions, regional bodies, civil society, women and youth), sustained attention after the SC moves on, advice to the SC and GA, and resource mobilization.
  • The Peacebuilding Fund (2006) has allocated over $1.5 billion to dozens of countries; it is fast and risk-tolerant, often first money in. The GA agreed to give it some assessed funding from 2025.
  • Main criticism: no power or money of its own beyond the Fund; its influence depends on whether the SC listens to its advice.

Disarmament and non-proliferation

About 16 min

The NPT

  • Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons: opened for signature 1 July 1968, in force 5 March 1970, extended indefinitely in 1995. 191 states parties.
  • Three pillars: non-proliferation; disarmament (Art. VI: pursue negotiations in good faith); the right to peaceful nuclear energy (Art. IV).
  • Five recognized nuclear-weapon states (tested before 1 January 1967): the US, Russia, the UK, France and China, the same as the P5.
  • Outside the NPT: India, Pakistan and Israel (never joined) and South Sudan. The DPRK announced its withdrawal in 2003. Iran is a party.
  • Review Conferences every five years. The 2026 RevCon (New York, April–May 2026) ended on 22 May without a consensus final document, the third failure in a row after 2015 and 2022. The last successful outcome was in 2010.

The IAEA

  • International Atomic Energy Agency, founded 1957 after Eisenhower's "Atoms for Peace" speech (1953). Based in Vienna; Director-General Rafael Grossi (Argentina) since 2019. Nobel Peace Prize 2005.
  • An independent related organization that reports to the GA and, on safeguards violations, to the SC.
  • Organs: the General Conference (all members, every September) and the Board of Governors (35 members, the real decision-maker).
  • Jobs: safeguards (verifying nuclear material is not diverted to weapons; the Additional Protocol allows stronger inspections), nuclear safety and security, and technical cooperation (nuclear medicine, agriculture, water: "Atoms for Peace and Development").
  • Nuclear plants in conflict: since 2022 the IAEA has kept experts at Zaporizhzhia (Ukraine) and set out seven indispensable pillars and five concrete principles for nuclear safety and security in war.
  • Iran: the 2015 deal (JCPOA) collapsed; Israel and the United States struck Iranian nuclear sites in June 2025; Iran suspended cooperation with the IAEA; UN sanctions were reimposed through the "snapback" process in September 2025.

The IAEA in more depth

  • Founded 1957 under its own Statute; 180+ member states; reports annually to the GA and, on safeguards non-compliance, to the SC.
  • Board of Governors (35 members) makes most decisions (e.g. finding Iran in non-compliance); the General Conference (all members) meets each September and approves the budget.
  • Safeguards: comprehensive safeguards agreements for NPT non-nuclear states; the Additional Protocol (1997) allows short-notice inspections of undeclared sites.
  • Nuclear security in war: Grossi's seven indispensable pillars (2022) include physical integrity of facilities, working safety systems, staff able to work without pressure, secure off-site power, supply chains, radiation monitoring and communication with the regulator. IAEA teams have been stationed at Ukraine's nuclear plants since 2022.
  • Development: technical cooperation in cancer care (Rays of Hope), food and agriculture (Atoms4Food with the FAO), zoonotic disease (ZODIAC), water and plastic pollution (NUTEC Plastics).

Iran in brief

The 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (endorsed by S/RES/2231) limited enrichment in exchange for sanctions relief. The US left in 2018; Iran then expanded enrichment to 60%. In June 2025 Israel and the US struck Iranian nuclear facilities; Iran suspended cooperation with the IAEA. The E3 (France, Germany, UK) triggered the "snapback" and UN sanctions returned in late September 2025.

Conventional arms and new domains

  • Small arms: the UN Programme of Action (2001) and the Arms Trade Treaty (2013).
  • Landmines and cluster munitions: Ottawa Convention (1997), Convention on Cluster Munitions (2008).
  • Outer space: Outer Space Treaty (1967) bans nuclear weapons in orbit; talks on preventing an arms race in outer space (PAROS).
  • Cyberspace: GA open-ended working groups on ICT security since 2019 agreed non-binding norms of responsible state behaviour and a permanent mechanism.
  • Autonomous weapons: CCW Group of Governmental Experts; GA resolutions since 2023; calls for a treaty.

SDGs, development finance and climate

About 17 min

From MDGs to SDGs

  • Millennium Development Goals: eight goals, 2000–2015, from the Millennium Declaration. They halved extreme poverty but were criticized as donor-driven, aimed only at developing countries, and silent on environment, inequality and governance.
  • 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development: A/RES/70/1, adopted 25 September 2015. 17 goals, 169 targets, universal (applies to all countries), "leave no one behind".
  • Follow-up: the HLPF (ECOSOC every July; GA-level SDG Summit every four years: 2019, 2023, next 2027), with Voluntary National Reviews. The 2023 Summit found only about 15% of targets on track.

Financing for development

  • Conferences: Monterrey (2002), Doha (2008), Addis Ababa Action Agenda (2015), and the 4th conference (FfD4) in Sevilla, Spain (30 June–3 July 2025), which adopted the Compromiso de Sevilla (the United States walked out of the process).
  • The SDG financing gap for developing countries is estimated at over $4 trillion a year.
  • Recurring issues: debt distress (many low-income countries spend more on debt service than on health or education), reform of the IMF and World Bank, tax cooperation (a UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation is being negotiated), illicit financial flows, and the 0.7% of GNI aid target that most donors miss. Aid fell sharply in 2025 as major donors cut budgets.
  • Groupings: G77 and China (about 134 developing states, the main negotiating bloc), Least Developed Countries (LDCs), Small Island Developing States (SIDS), Landlocked Developing Countries (LLDCs).

In depth

The 2030 Agenda in more depth

  • Built around the "5 Ps": People, Planet, Prosperity, Peace, Partnership; pledge to "leave no one behind".
  • Negotiated by all member states (the MDGs were drafted largely by UN staff), after an Open Working Group of the GA proposed the goals.
  • Indicators (about 230) are set by the UN Statistical Commission; progress is tracked in the SG's annual SDG Report.
  • Follow-up: the HLPF reviews a set of goals each year in depth; states present VNRs voluntarily (most states have done at least one).

The eight MDGs and why they fell short

(1) End extreme poverty and hunger; (2) universal primary education; (3) gender equality; (4) reduce child mortality; (5) improve maternal health; (6) combat HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases; (7) environmental sustainability; (8) global partnership for development. Successes: extreme poverty roughly halved (largely thanks to China and India), primary enrolment and child survival rose. Weaknesses: designed top-down, applied only to developing countries, silent on inequality, governance, peace and climate, and uneven progress, especially in sub-Saharan Africa and conflict-affected states.

Climate in more depth

  • IPCC (1988, set up by WMO and UNEP) assesses the science; its 2021–23 Sixth Assessment found human influence on warming "unequivocal".
  • Rio Earth Summit (1992) produced the Rio Declaration, Agenda 21, the UNFCCC, the CBD and the process leading to the UNCCD (1994).
  • Kyoto (1997): binding targets for developed (Annex I) countries only; the US never ratified; second period agreed in Doha (2012).
  • Copenhagen (COP15, 2009) failed to agree a successor, but produced the pledge of $100 billion a year in climate finance by 2020 (reached only in 2022).
  • Paris (2015): bottom-up NDCs from all parties, a "ratchet" to raise ambition every five years, transparency rules, adaptation goals, carbon markets (Art. 6) and loss and damage (Art. 8).
  • Recent COPs: COP26 Glasgow (2021, "phase down" coal); COP27 Sharm el-Sheikh (2022, hosted by Egypt: agreed to create a loss and damage fund); COP28 Dubai (2023, first Global Stocktake: "transitioning away from fossil fuels", fund made operational); COP29 Baku (2024, $300 billion a year by 2035); COP30 Belém (2025); COP31 Antalya (November 2026).
  • The US withdrew from the Paris Agreement again (effective January 2026).

Other environmental milestones

  • Montreal Protocol (1987) on ozone-depleting substances: universally ratified, often called the most successful environmental treaty.
  • Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022): protect 30% of land and sea by 2030 ("30x30").
  • High Seas Treaty (BBNJ), adopted 2023, in force January 2026: marine protected areas in waters beyond national jurisdiction.
  • Plastics treaty negotiations (since 2022) failed to agree a text in 2024 and 2025.

Country and conference skills

Speeches and resolution writing

About 8 min

A 60–90 second speech

  1. Hook (one sentence): a striking fact. "Every two minutes, a woman dies from preventable causes related to pregnancy."
  2. Stance: "[Country] firmly believes…"
  3. Evidence: one framework and one national action.
  4. Proposal: one or two concrete ideas for the committee.
  5. Call to cooperation: "We invite all delegations, particularly…, to work with us on…"

Speak to be remembered for one idea. Pause between sections. Use the country's name, not "I".

Resolution format

A resolution is one long sentence: the committee name, then preambulatory clauses (why: context, past actions; ending in commas) and operative clauses (what: numbered actions; ending in semicolons, the last with a period).

Example

The General Assembly,

Recalling Article 19 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights,

Deeply concerned by the rising number of journalists killed in the line of duty,

1. Calls upon Member States to establish national protection mechanisms for journalists, drawing on the UN Plan of Action on the Safety of Journalists and the Issue of Impunity;

2. Requests the Secretary-General to report on the implementation of this resolution at its eighty-third session.

What makes a clause strong

  • Who acts (member states, UNESCO, OHCHR), what exactly, how it is funded, and how progress is checked (a report back).
  • Build on existing bodies rather than creating new ones; new bodies cost money that the Fifth Committee must approve.
  • Respect sovereignty: "encourage" and "invite" states; offer technical assistance rather than imposing.
