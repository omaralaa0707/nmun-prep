**How to use it in the interview:** poverty, debt and climate shocks explain why Mozambique asks for development finance, debt relief and burden-sharing (UNHCR), and why it frames the Cabo Delgado insurgency as rooted in exclusion from resource wealth (GA1, PMSCs).

#### Geographical significance

- **Coastline of about 2,500 km** on the Indian Ocean and the **Mozambique Channel** (between it and Madagascar), a major shipping lane between Asia and the Atlantic.

- **Six land neighbours:** Tanzania (north), Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe (west), South Africa and Eswatini (south). Mnemonic: "TMZ-ZSE".

- **Gateway for landlocked neighbours.** Its ports at **Maputo, Beira and Nacala** sit at the end of transport corridors for Malawi, Zambia and Zimbabwe. That makes Mozambique strategically important to all of southern Africa.

- **Great rivers:** the Zambezi and Limpopo. The **Cahora Bassa dam** on the Zambezi exports electricity to South Africa.

- **Resources:** the offshore **Rovuma Basin gas fields** off Cabo Delgado (among the largest in Africa), coal at Moatize (Tete), the world's largest ruby deposit at Montepuez, graphite at Balama, heavy sands, and the Mozal aluminium smelter near Maputo (on care and maintenance since **March 2026**, after South32 could not agree an affordable power deal; about 4,000 jobs lost).

- **Climate exposure:** one of the most cyclone- and flood-prone countries in the world. Cyclones **Idai** and Kenneth (2019), **Freddy** (2023), and **Chido** (December 2024, hit Cabo Delgado), Dikeledi and Jude (2025). Disasters regularly displace hundreds of thousands, on top of the conflict.

#### History in six steps

- **Portuguese colony** from the 1500s. The **Island of Mozambique** was the capital until 1898 (UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1991).

- **FRELIMO** (Mozambique Liberation Front) founded in 1962 in Dar es Salaam under **Eduardo Mondlane** (assassinated 1969). Armed liberation struggle from 1964.

- **Independence 25 June 1975** after Portugal's 1974 Carnation Revolution and the Lusaka Accord. First president **Samora Machel** (killed in a 1986 plane crash in South Africa).

- **Civil war 1977–1992** between the FRELIMO government and **RENAMO**, which was backed first by Rhodesia, then by apartheid South Africa. About a million dead and millions displaced, including over 1.5 million refugees in neighbouring countries.

- **Rome General Peace Accords, 4 October 1992**, mediated by the Catholic Community of Sant'Egidio. The UN peacekeeping mission **ONUMOZ** (1992–94) oversaw demobilisation and the 1994 elections; it is often cited as a UN success.

- Renewed RENAMO fighting 2013–2019, ended by the **Maputo Accord (6 August 2019)**; disarmament of RENAMO fighters completed in 2023. Presidents since 1975: Machel, Chissano, Guebuza, Nyusi (2015–2025), Chapo (2025–).

#### Foreign policy principles

- **Non-alignment and "friends with all":** close historic ties with Russia and China (Soviet-era support for FRELIMO; Russian FM Lavrov visited Maputo in July 2026), while working closely with the EU, the US, Portugal and Rwanda. Mozambique abstained on the 2022 GA resolution condemning Russia's invasion of Ukraine.

- **African solutions and regional solidarity:** a founding member of SADC (via SADCC, 1980) and a strong believer in regional security cooperation, shown by SAMIM.

- **Sovereignty first, but welcomes partners:** Mozambique invites help on its terms (Rwanda, SADC, the EU) and resists outside conditions.

- **Peace through dialogue:** it is proud of the 1992 and 2019 peace accords and of ONUMOZ, so it argues for political solutions, reconciliation and reintegration.

- **Development is security:** the official line on Cabo Delgado pairs military action with jobs, services and the Northern Integrated Development Agency (ADIN, created 2020).

#### Groupings and UN record

- **UN regional group:** African States. Also: African Union, **SADC**, the Group of 77 and China, Non-Aligned Movement, LDCs, **Commonwealth** (1995; the first member never ruled by Britain), the Community of Portuguese Language Countries (CPLP, founding member 1996), the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, Indian Ocean Rim Association.

- **Security Council: elected member 2023–2024**, its first term ever. Priorities included counter-terrorism, women, peace and security, and African peace operations.

- Hosted **ONUMOZ** (1992–94), one of the UN's peacekeeping success stories.

- **Disarmament credentials:** declared **free of known landmines in 2015** after decades of clearance; party to the Mine Ban Treaty, the Convention on Cluster Munitions (2011), the Chemical Weapons Convention (2000), the NPT and the African nuclear-weapon-free-zone Treaty of Pelindaba. **Not** a party to the CCW (the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, where autonomous weapons are negotiated).

- **Refugee law:** 1951 Refugee Convention (acceded 1983, **with reservations** on several articles, including freedom of movement), the 1967 Protocol, the OAU Refugee Convention (1969), the **Kampala Convention** on internally displaced persons (ratified 2017), and its own **Policy and Strategy for the Management of Internally Displaced Persons (2021)**.

- **Private military:** not a participant in the Montreux Document and not a party to the 1989 UN Mercenaries Convention (per Mozambican civil society, 2020).

#### How to sound like Mozambique

> Example answer: "Mozambique speaks from experience. We have lived through a liberation war, a civil war, a peace process the UN helped deliver, and since 2017 an insurgency in Cabo Delgado that has displaced over a million of our people. That is why we believe security, accountability and development cannot be separated."

Three themes to repeat in both committees: **lived experience** (we know this first-hand), **African and regional ownership** (SADC, the AU, partnership on our terms), and **capacity and fair burden-sharing** (frameworks must come with the means for developing states to implement them).

## Mozambique in GA1 and UNHCR: its record and statements

The guidelines ask for Mozambique's "foreign policy towards your two committee preferences". This unit is built from Mozambique's **own statements and votes**, so you can quote it directly. Where a position is inferred instead, the topic units say so.

#### How Mozambique speaks at the UN

- Almost every statement opens by aligning with groups: **"Mozambique aligns itself with the statements delivered on behalf of the African Group, the Non-Aligned Movement, and the Group of 77 and China"**, sometimes also the **Community of Portuguese-Speaking Countries (CPLP)**, "and wishes to add the following remarks in its national capacity".

- Then it adds national experience: landmines, the civil war, Cabo Delgado, cyclones, refugees.

- Recurring themes: **disarmament is a development issue**, **capacity-building for developing countries**, **African and regional frameworks**, and **multilateralism**.

- Use the same formula in the interview: "Mozambique aligns with the African Group, and in its national capacity stresses..."

#### Mozambique in GA1: its own words (October 2025)

**Conventional weapons debate, 23 October 2025** (aligned with the African Group, NAM and CPLP):

- "This year, Mozambique marks **ten years since being declared landmine-free**, a national achievement built upon global solidarity and a reminder of the enduring human cost of conventional weapons."

- Small arms: while producing and trafficking weapons is cheap, destroying them and rehabilitating communities is "prohibitively high, particularly for developing countries. **Disarmament, therefore, is not only a security goal, it is a development imperative.**"

- Full commitment to the **UN Programme of Action on small arms** and the **International Tracing Instrument**; strong support for a **UN Fellowship Programme on small arms for developing countries** (an African Group priority); links to the **SADC Protocol**, the Nairobi and Bamako Declarations and **"Silencing the Guns in Africa"**.

- **On autonomous weapons (your topic):** "We are witnessing a dangerous convergence between conventional weapons and emerging technologies, including **autonomous systems, armed drones, and artificial intelligence-enabled targeting**." These "low-cost innovations magnify lethality and accessibility while escaping traditional arms-control regimes." Mozambique "supports renewed global dialogue... and **the need to preserve human control and accountability over all uses of force**."

**Other weapons of mass destruction debate, 22 October 2025:**

- Warned about the convergence of terrorism, WMD and emerging technology ("AI-driven biodesign, 3D printing, and unregulated drone technology") and the threat from **non-State actors**.

- Urged full implementation of **Security Council resolution 1540**. During its Security Council term, Mozambique **co-hosted with UNODA and the 1540 Committee the 3rd Training Course for National Focal Points in Africa** (Addis Ababa, 5–7 November 2024, 42 states), praised by High Representative Nakamitsu.

- Called for a **legally binding verification mechanism for the Biological Weapons Convention**, African centres of excellence on chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear risks, and "**algorithmic accountability and AI safeguards**" in future WMD rules "to prevent the automation of destruction".

## GA First Committee: full committee sheet

This is the full committee sheet the guidelines ask for: mandate, functions, powers and **subsidiary entities**, plus the current session, the people, the money and the controversies. It follows the same structure as a strong HRC sheet.

#### Identity at a glance

| Item | Fact |
| --- | --- |
| Full name | First Committee of the General Assembly: **Disarmament and International Security Committee (DISEC)** |
| Type | One of the GA's **six Main Committees** (First: disarmament; Second: economic and financial; Third: social, humanitarian and cultural; Fourth: special political and decolonisation; Fifth: administrative and budgetary; Sixth: legal) |
| Membership | All **193** member states, one vote each. Observer states (the Holy See, the State of Palestine) and the EU can speak but not vote. |
| Where and when | New York; every autumn, roughly early October to early November (about **4–5 weeks**) |
| Secretariat support | **UN Office for Disarmament Affairs (UNODA)** for substance, and the Department for General Assembly and Conference Management for meetings |
| Output | About **60–70** draft resolutions and decisions a year, sent to the GA plenary for final adoption in early December |
| Legal force | **Recommendations only**: not binding on states |

#### Bureau of the 81st session (2026, meeting now)

- Vice-Chairs: Faisal Abdelazim Salim Mohamed (Sudan), Arsen Kotanjyan (Armenia) and one more

- Rapporteur: Kim Van der Sluis (Netherlands)

These names change every year. Knowing the current chair shows you follow the committee in real time; if unsure in the interview, say "the 81st session is chaired by Nepal".

#### History of the committee

- **1945–46:** created as the **Political and Security Committee**, handling all political and security items, including disarmament.

- **First GA resolution ever, 1(I) of January 1946**, set up the UN Atomic Energy Commission to deal with atomic weapons: disarmament was on the agenda from day one.

- **1978:** after the first **Special Session on Disarmament (SSOD-I)**, the First Committee was limited to **disarmament and related international security questions**. Other political items moved to the Special Political Committee (merged with the Fourth Committee in 1993).

- Later special sessions: SSOD-II (1982) and SSOD-III (1988) ended without agreed outcomes. A fourth special session (SSOD-IV) has been discussed for decades but never convened.

#### Mandate

**The First Committee deals with disarmament, global challenges and threats to peace that affect the international community, and seeks solutions to the challenges in the international security regime.** It considers all disarmament and international security matters within the scope of the Charter or relating to the powers and functions of any other UN organ.

**Charter basis:**

- **Article 11(1):** the GA "may consider the general principles of co-operation in the maintenance of international peace and security, including the principles governing disarmament and the regulation of armaments, and may make recommendations".

- **Article 11(2):** it may discuss any question on peace and security brought by a member, the Security Council or a non-member.

- **Article 12:** it may not make recommendations on a dispute the Security Council is currently dealing with (unless the Council asks).

- **Article 26:** the **Security Council** is responsible for plans to regulate armaments. GA1 sets norms; the Council acts.

- **Article 13:** the GA encourages the progressive development and codification of international law, which is why GA1 can launch treaty work.

**Concerned with a certain group of people?** No. It concerns all states, and increasingly human security (civilians, victims, survivors).

#### Functions and powers

- **Debate and recommend:** adopt resolutions setting principles and norms (nuclear disarmament, small arms, autonomous weapons, outer space, ICT security).

- **Request reports:** ask the Secretary-General to collect states' views and report (A/RES/78/241 on autonomous weapons led to report A/79/88 in 2024).

- **Create bodies and processes:** Groups of Governmental Experts (GGEs, a limited number of experts), Open-Ended Working Groups (OEWGs, open to all states), informal consultations, scientific panels, studies.

- **Launch and adopt treaties:** mandate negotiations and recommend finished treaties for adoption, for example the **Arms Trade Treaty** (process launched 2006, adopted by the GA 2013), the **Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons** (negotiated under a GA mandate, 2017), and the **Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty** (adopted by the GA 1996).

- **Transparency tools:** run the **UN Register of Conventional Arms** (1991) and the **UN Report on Military Expenditures** (1980), where states voluntarily report.

- **Convene conferences and special sessions**, including the conference on a Middle East zone free of nuclear and other weapons of mass destruction (held annually since 2019).

**What it cannot do:** bind states, impose sanctions or arms embargoes, authorise the use of force, deploy missions or inspect facilities. Those belong to the Security Council and to treaty bodies like the IAEA and OPCW.

#### How a session works

- **General debate** (about eight meetings): each state, and groups such as the African Group, NAM and the EU, sets out its priorities.

- **Thematic discussion** (about two weeks) in **seven clusters**:
  

  Nuclear weapons

  - Other weapons of mass destruction (chemical, biological)

  - Outer space (disarmament aspects)

  - **Conventional weapons** (both your topics: private military companies and autonomous weapons, plus small arms, landmines, cluster munitions, ammunition)

  - Other disarmament measures and international security (ICT, AI, gender, development)

  - Regional disarmament and security

  - Disarmament machinery

  

  Panels with the High Representative, heads of treaty bodies and experts take place in this phase.
- **Action on all drafts** (about a week): votes on resolutions and decisions, explanations of vote, and rights of reply.

Drafts are tabled by a **main sponsor** (often a state or group with a long-standing interest: Austria on autonomous weapons, Japan on nuclear disarmament, Mexico and Ireland on humanitarian disarmament, the NAM on many nuclear texts) with co-sponsors. Many resolutions are "biennialised" or "triennialised" (taken up every two or three years) to save time.

#### Voting and decisions

- Each member has **one vote**.

- In the committee, decisions are taken by a **simple majority of members present and voting** (abstentions do not count).

- Many texts are adopted **without a vote** (by consensus), but the share is shrinking. Nakamitsu told the 81st session that "increasing numbers of votes and rights of reply" show a more divided committee.

- States can request a **recorded vote** (each state's vote is listed), separate votes on individual paragraphs, and give **explanations of vote**.

- In the plenary, Charter **Article 18(2)** lists "recommendations with respect to the maintenance of international peace and security" among "important questions" needing a **two-thirds majority**.

- Any **programme budget implications** (costs of a new process) go to the Fifth Committee before plenary adoption.

#### Key UN organs relevant to GA1

- **Security Council:** binding arms embargoes, sanctions, the 1540 regime, peacekeeping disarmament (DDR) mandates. Russia vetoed a 2024 Council resolution on keeping nuclear weapons out of orbit.

- **GA plenary and Fourth Committee:** peaceful uses of outer space (COPUOS) go to the Fourth Committee; GA1 covers the military side.

- **Human Rights Council:** the intergovernmental working group on private military and security companies and the Working Group on the use of mercenaries.

- **Peacebuilding Commission and DPO:** disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration (DDR) of fighters, as in Mozambique with RENAMO (completed 2023).

- **Secretary-General:** his 2018 disarmament agenda "Securing Our Common Future" and the 2023 **New Agenda for Peace**, which called for a treaty on autonomous weapons by 2026.

#### Recent priorities and the 81st session (2026)

From the High Representative's opening speech (6 October 2026), the three main challenges:

- **Nuclear risk** "higher than at any time since the Cold War". The last US–Russia arms control treaty (**New START**) has **expired**, so for the first time in fifty years there are no caps on the two largest arsenals. Three NPT Review Conferences in a row have failed.

- **Record military spending:** **$2.887 trillion in 2025**, the highest ever, up **41% in ten years**, while the SDGs fall behind.

- **Military AI:** AI and autonomy are spreading across armed forces. Informal exchanges on AI in the military domain, created by GA1, have begun, and Nakamitsu called for a dedicated multilateral forum. She noted **agreed elements for an instrument on lethal autonomous weapons** as a recent success.

Other current themes: confidence-building measures (TCBMs), drones and missiles, the protection of civilians (the 2022 Political Declaration on Explosive Weapons in Populated Areas), gender in disarmament, youth engagement, and withdrawals from humanitarian disarmament treaties.

#### Budget

- GA1 has no budget of its own; its meetings are paid for by the UN regular budget.

- UNODA is funded partly by the **regular budget** (assessed contributions) and heavily by **voluntary contributions and trust funds**, such as the UN Trust Facility Supporting Cooperation on Arms Regulation (UNSCAR) and the Saving Lives Entity (SALIENT) fund for small arms.

- Any new meeting or expert group created by a resolution carries **programme budget implications** that the Fifth Committee must approve. The UN's liquidity crisis and the **UN80 reform initiative** (2025) are squeezing disarmament work too.

#### Controversies and criticisms

- **Polarisation:** more recorded votes, rival resolutions (for example, competing texts on outer space from Russia and from Western states) and rights of reply.

- **"Talking shop" criticism:** GA1 cannot bind anyone, and the CD it relies on for negotiations has been stuck since 1996.

- **Nuclear divide:** TPNW supporters (most of the Global South, including Africa) versus nuclear-armed states and their allies, which reject it.

- **Treaty withdrawals:** European states leaving the landmine and cluster munition treaties, criticised as reversing humanitarian progress.

- **Consensus as a veto:** in the CCW and CD, one state can block everything, which is why many states push to move autonomous weapons talks to the GA.

- **Middle East WMD-free zone:** Israel and the US do not take part in the annual conference.

#### Mozambique in GA1

- Aligns with the **African Group, the Non-Aligned Movement and the Group of 77 and China**; its October 2025 statement on conventional weapons aligned with all three.

- **Humanitarian disarmament record:** decades of landmine clearance ending with mine-free status in 2015; party to the Ottawa, cluster munitions, chemical weapons, NPT and Pelindaba treaties.

Mozambique is also bound by the **SADC Protocol on Firearms** (2001) and backs the AU's "Silencing the Guns" agenda.

- **Security Council 2023–24:** its first term, focused on counter-terrorism and African peace operations, which gives it standing on international security.

- Voted **yes on A/RES/80/57** (autonomous weapons, 2025).

## Topic: private military and security companies

**GA1 topic 1:** "Strengthening International Frameworks on the Activities of Private Military and Security Companies". This is your strongest topic: Mozambique is one of very few states to have hired both Russian and Western-linked contractors in the same conflict.

#### Definitions

- **PMSCs** (Montreux Document definition): "private business entities that provide military and/or security services, irrespective of how they describe themselves". Services include armed guarding and protection of people and objects, maintaining and operating weapons systems, detaining prisoners, and advising or training armed forces.

- **Mercenary** (Additional Protocol I, **Article 47**, 1977): someone who meets **six cumulative conditions**: specially recruited to fight; takes a direct part in hostilities; motivated by private gain and paid much more than regular soldiers; not a national or resident of a party; not a member of a party's armed forces; not sent officially by another state. Because all six must be met, almost no contractor qualifies. This is the core legal gap.

- **Inherently state functions**: tasks that should never be outsourced, above all direct participation in combat and detention. Where to draw this line is a central debate.

- Do not confuse: a **PMSC** (a company), a **mercenary** (a legal status), and **state-to-state deployments** (Rwanda in Mozambique), which are lawful when the host consents.

#### Why it matters

- **Scale:** in Iraq and Afghanistan, contractors at times outnumbered US troops. The industry is worth hundreds of billions of dollars a year.

- **Accountability gap:** contractors fall between national jurisdictions and the law of armed conflict. Victims rarely get remedies.

- **Landmark abuses:** Blackwater's **Nisour Square shooting**, Baghdad 2007 (17 Iraqi civilians killed); the **Moura massacre**, Mali, March 2022 (about 500 killed; the UN human rights office implicated Malian troops and "foreign military personnel", widely reported as Wagner).

- **Africa as the main theatre:** Wagner in the Central African Republic (from 2018), Sudan, Libya (2019–20), **Mozambique (2019)** and Mali (from 2021). After Prigozhin's death in August 2023, Wagner's African operations were folded into Russia's state-run **Africa Corps**; Wagner left Mali in 2025 and Africa Corps stayed. Deals often trade security for mining concessions. Earlier: Executive Outcomes in Angola and Sierra Leone (1990s).

- The UN itself uses private security companies to guard premises, under its 2012 guidelines.

#### The existing frameworks (know all six)

| **Additional Protocol I, Art. 47** | 1977 | Binding; mercenaries lose combatant and prisoner-of-war status. Definition too narrow. |
| **International Convention against the Recruitment, Use, Financing and Training of Mercenaries** | 1989 (GA res. 44/34; in force 2001) | Binding, but ratified by only a few dozen states and no major military power. Mozambique is not a party. |
| **Montreux Document** | 17 September 2008 (Switzerland and the ICRC) | **Non-binding.** Restates existing legal obligations of states and lists about 70 good practices for contracting, home and territorial states. | **International Code of Conduct for Private Security Service Providers (ICoC)** | 2010; oversight body ICoCA created 2013 (Geneva) | Voluntary industry self-regulation; companies sign on and get certified. |

#### Mozambique's own case (your best material)

- **Wagner Group, from September 2019.** About 200 Russian contractors with helicopters deployed to Cabo Delgado. They did not know the terrain, clashed with Mozambican troops, suffered deadly ambushes and pulled back within months.

- **Dyck Advisory Group (DAG), April 2020 to April 2021.** A South African company founded by former Zimbabwean colonel Lionel Dyck, flying helicopter gunships. Amnesty International's March 2021 report **"What I Saw Is Death"** (based on 79 interviews with displaced people) accused DAG of firing indiscriminately into crowds, alongside abuses by government forces and by the insurgents.

- Other private firms (Paramount, Burnham Global) supplied equipment and training.

- **The shift to states:** after the Palma attack, Mozambique turned to **Rwanda** (July 2021, bilateral) and **SADC's SAMIM** (July 2021 to July 2024), with EU training. They retook Mocímboa da Praia in August 2021.

**The lesson Mozambique draws:** private contractors failed militarily and left an accountability gap, while state and regional deployments under clear mandates worked better. Contractors are a symptom of weak state capacity; the real answer is capable, accountable national forces and regional cooperation.

#### Mozambique's position (how to present it)

Mozambique has not published a detailed position on this topic. This is the policy that follows from its record and its alignment with the African Group. Present it confidently, but if pushed, say it is built on Mozambique's experience and the African Group's stance.

- **Supports a legally binding international framework**, developed through the Human Rights Council's intergovernmental working group, with GA1 giving political backing.

- **Clear limits:** no outsourcing of direct participation in hostilities or detention.

- **Home-state responsibility:** countries where PMSCs are registered must license, vet and prosecute them, so host states like Mozambique are not left alone.

- **Remedies for victims**, including in the country where the harm happened.

- **Capacity and security sector reform** so states do not need contractors: training, funding (for example the AU–UN financing model in S/RES/2719 of 2023), and regional forces like SAMIM.

- **Respect for sovereignty:** states may seek lawful security help, but rules must apply equally to all providers, Russian, Western or anyone else.

#### Possible resolution ideas

- Call on the HRC working group to finalise a draft binding instrument by a set date.

- Encourage states to join the Montreux Document and require ICoCA membership in all their contracts as an interim step (Mozambique could announce that it is considering joining).

- A voluntary UN registry of PMSCs and contracts, run by UNODA or OHCHR.

- Model national legislation and licensing, with UNREC training for African states.

- Updating the 1977 OAU Convention through the African Union.

#### Tough questions to expect

- **"Mozambique hired Wagner. Why should anyone listen to you?"** Because we learned the hard way. We have seen what happens without rules, and that is exactly why we want them. Own it; don't dodge.

- **"Mozambique hasn't joined the Montreux Document or the 1989 Convention."** Acknowledge it: both have gaps (non-binding; outdated definition), which is why Mozambique wants a single modern framework, and joining Montreux is a reasonable step meanwhile.

- **"Is Rwanda's deployment any different from Wagner?"** Yes: it is a state-to-state deployment at Mozambique's request, by a state's armed forces under its chain of command and accountable under international law. Contractors are accountable to a client and shareholders.

- **"What can GA1 do if the treaty is negotiated in the HRC?"** Give political direction, ask for Secretary-General reports, connect the human rights and security tracks, and build the majority a treaty needs.

## Topic: lethal autonomous weapon systems

**GA1 topic 2:** "Lethal Autonomous Weapon Systems and the Preservation of Meaningful Human Control". Mozambique has no special record here, so your strength must be **knowing the process precisely** and speaking for the African and Global South view.

#### Definitions

- **Lethal autonomous weapon systems (LAWS)**: there is **no agreed definition**. The ICRC's working definition: weapons that **select and apply force to targets without human intervention**. After a person activates them, they strike based on what their sensors detect matching a "target profile". The US definition (Directive 3000.09, updated 2023): once activated, can select and engage targets without further human intervention.

- **Automatic versus autonomous**: landmines and defensive systems like the Phalanx gun or Iron Dome react automatically in narrow situations; the concern is systems that search for and choose targets across time and space, increasingly using AI.

- **Human in, on or out of the loop**: a human decides each strike (in), supervises and can stop it (on), or is absent (out).

- **Meaningful human control**: the idea that people must keep enough understanding, judgement and control over a weapon to be responsible for its effects. It has no agreed definition either, but is the heart of the topic.

#### Why it matters

- **International humanitarian law:** can a machine apply **distinction** (civilians versus fighters), **proportionality** and **precaution**? The **Martens Clause** adds "principles of humanity and the dictates of public conscience".

- **Accountability gap:** if a machine kills unlawfully, who is responsible: the commander, programmer or manufacturer?

- **Human dignity:** a machine should not decide who lives or dies.

- **Proliferation:** cheap drones and software spread to armed groups. Islamic State affiliates already use commercial drones.

- **Lower threshold for war** and fast escalation when no soldiers are at risk.

- **Bias and error:** algorithms trained on limited data may misidentify people, with darker-skinned people and the Global South most at risk.

- **Real-world use:** a 2021 UN Panel of Experts report on **Libya** (S/2021/229) said Turkish-made **Kargu-2** drones "hunted down" retreating forces in 2020, possibly without a human link. AI-assisted targeting has been reported in Ukraine, Gaza and, in 2026, Iran.

#### The "two-tier" approach (where most states are converging)

- **Prohibit** systems that cannot be used in line with IHL, or that operate without meaningful human control (for example, those whose effects cannot be predicted, or that target people directly).

- **Regulate** all other autonomous systems: limits on targets, duration, area and context; human supervision and the ability to deactivate; testing and legal reviews (Article 36 of Additional Protocol I).

| CCW only, by consensus | Many European states, Japan, South Korea, Australia | Support an instrument but in the CCW, where all major military powers participate |
| No new law | US, Russia, India, Israel | Existing IHL is enough; prefer non-binding principles and national reviews. Russia argues the issue is premature. |
| Narrow ban | China | Backs banning "unacceptable" fully autonomous systems under a very narrow definition; abstained on A/RES/80/57 |

#### Mozambique's position

- **Voted yes** on A/RES/80/57 and aligns with the African Group, NAM and G77 statements in the First Committee (its October 2025 national statement aligned with all three).

- **Its own words** (First Committee, 23 October 2025): "a dangerous convergence between conventional weapons and emerging technologies, including autonomous systems, armed drones, and artificial intelligence-enabled targeting"; Mozambique supports "the need to **preserve human control and accountability over all uses of force**". On 22 October 2025 it also called for "algorithmic accountability and AI safeguards" to "prevent the automation of destruction".

- The African Group calls for a **new legally binding instrument** with prohibitions and regulations that ensures **meaningful human control** over the use of force.

- **Mozambique's own angles:**
  

  As a country fighting an insurgency, it fears these weapons spreading to **non-state armed groups**.

  - As a developing country, it does not want Africa to become a **testing ground** (cite Libya 2020), and it insists on inclusive talks, not rules written only by producer states.

  - It knows the long human cost of indiscriminate weapons: it spent decades clearing landmines and was declared **mine-free in 2015**. "We know what it costs when weapons act without human judgement."

  - It supports **international cooperation and capacity building** so developing states can take part in technical talks.

  

- **Honest gap:** Mozambique is **not a party to the CCW**. Turn it into a point: this is why Mozambique wants the GA, where all 193 states sit, to stay involved, and why it could consider joining the CCW.

#### Possible resolution ideas

- Urge the CCW Review Conference to adopt a negotiating mandate, and if it fails, convene negotiations under the GA.

- Endorse the two-tier approach and a working definition of meaningful human control.

- Call for national legal weapons reviews and information sharing, with UNIDIR support.

- Capacity-building fund so developing states can participate.

- Measures against proliferation to non-state actors.

#### Tough questions to expect

- **"Why does Mozambique care? You don't build these weapons."** Because we may be where they are used, by states or by armed groups like the one we fight in Cabo Delgado. Rules made only by producers protect producers.

- **"Isn't existing IHL enough?"** IHL applies, but it was written for human decision-makers; the accountability gap and the lack of a definition show we need specific rules. Even the 11 Guiding Principles admit human responsibility cannot be transferred to machines.

- **"CCW or GA?"** Prefer the CCW if it delivers a negotiating mandate in November 2026; if it stays blocked by consensus, the GA must act. Mozambique, not being a CCW party, has a direct interest in the GA track.

#### History in brief

- **League of Nations era:** Fridtjof Nansen became the first High Commissioner for Refugees in 1921 (for Russian refugees) and created the **Nansen passport** (1922). The Nansen International Office for Refugees won the 1938 Nobel Peace Prize.

- **1940s:** UNRRA (1943) and the **International Refugee Organization** (1946–1952), which resettled over a million displaced Europeans.

- **1950–51:** UNHCR created with a **three-year mandate** and a tiny budget, mainly for Europe; the 1951 Convention adopted.

- **1960s–70s:** crises spread to Africa and Asia; the **1967 Protocol** made refugee law universal; the 1969 OAU Convention widened the definition in Africa.

- **1990s:** UNHCR moves into war zones (Bosnia, Rwanda) and begins helping IDPs and returnees. **1992–96: about 1.7 million Mozambican refugees came home** from Malawi and other neighbours after the Rome peace accords, then the largest repatriation UNHCR had organised in Africa.

- **2003:** GA removes the time limit on the mandate.

- **2005:** humanitarian reform and the cluster approach give UNHCR lead roles for IDPs.

- **2016–18:** New York Declaration (A/RES/71/1, 2016) and the **Global Compact on Refugees** (2018).

- **2025–26:** the worst funding crisis in its history; budget and staff cut sharply.

#### Mandate

**UNHCR's mandate is to provide international protection to refugees and to seek permanent solutions to their problems** (Statute, paragraph 1). Its work is "of an entirely non-political character" and "humanitarian and social" (paragraph 2).

- **Protection:** making sure refugees can access asylum, are not returned to danger (non-refoulement), and enjoy their rights in the host country.

- **Durable solutions:** **voluntary repatriation** in safety and dignity, **local integration** (including naturalisation) in the host country, or **resettlement** to a third country. Complementary pathways (study, work, family visas) supplement them.

- **Supervision:** under **Article 35** of the 1951 Convention (and Article II of the Protocol) states must cooperate with UNHCR, which supervises how they apply the Convention.

- **Expansion by the GA:** Statute paragraph 9 lets the GA assign extra tasks. On that basis UNHCR now covers **asylum seekers, returnees, stateless people** (GA mandates of 1974 and 1995) and **internally displaced persons** (with the consent of the state).

**Concerned with a certain group of people?** Yes: forcibly displaced and stateless people. That is the key difference from IOM (all migrants) and from the human rights bodies (everyone). **Palestine refugees** served by UNRWA are excluded under Article 1D of the Convention.

#### Functions and powers

- Promote the conclusion, ratification and application of refugee conventions.

- **Register refugees and decide refugee status** where states cannot or do not (mandate refugee status determination).

- Advise governments on asylum law and policy; issue legal guidelines (Handbook on Procedures and Criteria for Determining Refugee Status, 1979, updated).

- Deliver **emergency assistance**: shelter, core relief items, water, cash, health and education support, often with partners.

- Run or support camps and settlements, and increasingly support refugees living in cities.

- Lead the global **Protection Cluster** and co-lead Shelter and Camp Coordination and Camp Management for IDPs.

- Negotiate voluntary repatriation agreements (often tripartite: country of origin, host country, UNHCR) and refer refugees for resettlement.

- Prevent and reduce **statelessness**.

**Limits:** UNHCR cannot force a state to admit refugees, cannot operate without the host government's consent, has no enforcement powers, and depends almost entirely on voluntary funding.

- **Strategic Directions 2022–2026:** five core directions: **protect, respond, include, empower, solve**.

#### The numbers (Global Trends, June 2026)

- **117.8 million** forcibly displaced at the end of 2025, the **first fall in a decade** (from 123.2 million), about one in 70 people.

- **68.7 million IDPs**, **41.6 million refugees** (including 6 million Palestine refugees under UNRWA), plus asylum seekers and others in need of international protection.

- **14.7 million returned home in 2025**, the most UNHCR has recorded, largely to Syria and Afghanistan.

- Most refugees live in **low- and middle-income countries** next to their own, and many remain displaced for years (protracted situations).

- 2026 brought new displacement from the war involving Iran: over a million forced from their homes and 3.2 million internally displaced in Iran.

#### Recent priorities (77th ExCom, October 2026)

- Delivering protection with far less money: prioritising life-saving aid, cutting headquarters costs, closing or merging offices.

- **Solutions and returns**: supporting the record wave of returns while insisting they are voluntary, safe and dignified.

- **Burden- and responsibility-sharing** under the Global Compact, ahead of the 2027 Global Refugee Forum.

- Resolving **protracted refugee situations** (a support group of states made a joint statement at the 77th session).

- Inclusion of refugees in national systems (health, education, jobs) rather than parallel services.

#### Controversies and criticisms

- **Funding collapse:** donor cuts, led by the US, forced UNHCR to shed thousands of staff and close the Southern Africa bureau; critics warn protection is being sacrificed.

- **Resettlement collapse:** the US, historically the largest resettlement country, suspended its refugee admissions programme in January 2025.

- **Premature returns:** mass returns to Afghanistan (many forced from Iran and Pakistan) and to Syria after December 2024 raise doubts about whether returns are voluntary and safe.

- **Externalisation deals:** UNHCR criticised the UK–Rwanda plan (scrapped 2024) and has raised concerns about offshore processing such as Italy–Albania.

- **Refoulement by states:** pushbacks at Europe's borders and, in Africa, **Tanzania's forced return of over 10,000 Mozambicans in 2021**.

- **Encampment:** long-term camps (Dadaab and Kakuma in Kenya) criticised for keeping people dependent; the Global Compact pushes self-reliance instead.

- **Accountability:** a 2002 UNHCR and Save the Children report exposed sexual exploitation of refugee children by aid workers in West Africa, leading to strict UN rules on sexual exploitation and abuse.

- **IDP role:** debate over whether UNHCR should do more for IDPs (the majority of the displaced) or stay focused on refugees.

## Topic: psychosocial support for refugees and displaced persons

**UNHCR topic 1:** "Psychosocial Support for Refugees and Displaced Persons". The field is called **MHPSS**: mental health and psychosocial support.

#### Definitions

- **MHPSS** (IASC, 2007): "any type of local or outside support that aims to protect or promote psychosocial well-being and/or prevent or treat mental disorder".

- **Psychosocial**: the link between psychological effects (emotions, thoughts) and social factors (family, community, culture). Most distress after displacement is a normal reaction to abnormal events, not illness.

- **Mental disorder** versus distress: only some people need clinical care; everyone benefits from safety, dignity and community.

- **Task-shifting**: training non-specialists (nurses, community workers, teachers, refugees themselves) to deliver basic psychological help where specialists are scarce.

#### The IASC intervention pyramid

From the **Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Guidelines on MHPSS in Emergency Settings (2007)**, the key framework. Four layers, from everyone at the bottom to the few at the top:

- **Basic services and security** delivered in a dignified way (shelter, food, water, safety).

- **Community and family supports** (tracing families, child-friendly spaces, community activities, schooling).

- **Focused, non-specialised supports** (psychological first aid, basic counselling by trained non-specialists).

- **Specialised services** (psychiatrists, psychologists) for the small group with severe conditions.

Core principles: human rights and equity, participation, **do no harm**, build on existing resources, integrated services, multi-layered support.

#### The scale of the problem

- A WHO-supported study in *The Lancet* (2019) estimated that **about one in five people (22%)** in conflict-affected settings has depression, anxiety, PTSD, bipolar disorder or schizophrenia.

- Stress comes in three stages: **before flight** (violence, loss), **during the journey** (smuggling, detention, sexual violence) and **after arrival** (uncertainty, poverty, discrimination, no right to work).

- **Workforce gap:** low-income countries have fewer than two mental health workers per 100,000 people (WHO Mental Health Atlas), against dozens in rich countries.

- Children and adolescents, survivors of sexual and gender-based violence, former child soldiers, and people with disabilities are most at risk.

#### Main debates

- **Funding:** MHPSS is usually among the first services cut. With UNHCR only about 35% funded in 2026, programmes are shrinking.

- **Clinical versus community approaches:** Western trauma models (PTSD diagnosis, one-to-one therapy) versus community-based, culturally grounded support.

- **Integration:** stand-alone projects versus building MHPSS into health, education and protection services, and into national health systems that host communities also use.

- **Digital tools:** apps and tele-counselling can reach more people but raise privacy and access concerns.

- **Self-reliance:** the right to work and study is itself one of the strongest protections for mental health.

#### How UNHCR actually does MHPSS

- **Integrated, not stand-alone:** UNHCR's 2013 Operational Guidance says MHPSS belongs in **two sectors at once**: **health** (identifying and treating mental conditions in primary care) and **community-based protection** (rebuilding social support, preventing violence, helping people with disabilities and survivors).

- **Community-based protection:** refugee outreach volunteers, women's and youth groups, and refugee-led organisations identify people in distress and refer them.

- **Psychological First Aid** for all front-line staff, including registration and border staff.

- **mhGAP** training so general doctors and nurses in refugee clinics can treat depression, psychosis, epilepsy and substance use.

- **Scalable psychological interventions** from WHO (Problem Management Plus, Self-Help Plus, Step-by-Step digital self-help), tested in randomised trials with Syrian refugees and with South Sudanese refugees in Uganda.

- Monitoring through UNHCR's health information system, which records mental health consultations in camps.

- Staff care: humanitarian workers themselves face burnout and trauma.

#### Groups that need special attention

| Group | Why | What helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Children and adolescents** | About half of all refugees are children; violence, family separation, lost schooling, recruitment by armed groups | Child-friendly spaces, family tracing and reunification, safe schools, parenting support; UNICEF partnership |

| People in detention or long asylum procedures | Detention and uncertainty sharply worsen mental health | Alternatives to detention; faster, fair procedures |

#### Case studies

- **Rohingya in Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh** (about a million people): large community-based MHPSS programmes run by UN agencies and NGOs, with Rohingya volunteers as psychosocial workers; cuts in food aid have increased distress.

- **Syrian refugees in Türkiye, Lebanon and Jordan:** WHO's Problem Management Plus and Self-Help Plus were tested here; MHPSS was integrated into national primary health care.

- **Uganda:** its progressive refugee policy (freedom of movement, right to work, land for farming) is cited as a mental health protection in itself; Self-Help Plus was trialled with South Sudanese refugee women.

- **Ukraine (since 2022):** MHPSS made a national priority, with a national mental health programme championed by the First Lady.

- **Cabo Delgado, Mozambique:** UN agencies and NGOs run mobile psychosocial teams, child-friendly spaces and survivor services in IDP sites, but funding covers only a fraction of need.

- **Convention on the Rights of the Child, Article 39:** states must promote the physical and psychological recovery and reintegration of child victims of armed conflict and abuse.

#### Mozambique's angle

- **Cabo Delgado's trauma:** beheadings, burned villages, **abductions of children** (some forced to fight), sexual violence, and people displaced several times, then hit by cyclones. About 80% of the newly displaced are women and children.

- **Specialists are very scarce** in Mozambique, so it relies on community-based care and **task-shifting**, such as mid-level psychiatric technicians and trained community workers.

- **Experience after the civil war:** communities reintegrated former child soldiers partly through traditional cleansing and reconciliation ceremonies, combined with family reunification. This is a well-known example of culturally grounded psychosocial recovery.

- Host communities in Cabo Delgado and Nampula are as poor as the displaced, so services must serve both.

#### Mozambique's position (how to present it)

- MHPSS is **life-saving**, not optional, and should get a protected share of humanitarian appeals.

- **Community-based and culturally grounded** approaches, following the IASC pyramid; train teachers, health workers, religious leaders and displaced people themselves.

- **Integrate** MHPSS into national primary health care, so it serves refugees, IDPs and host communities and lasts after the emergency.

- Special focus on **children and survivors of sexual violence**: child-friendly spaces, reintegration of abducted children, safe schools.

- **Fair burden-sharing:** host and conflict-affected countries carry the load; donors must fund it predictably, including through pledges at the 2027 Global Refugee Forum.

- Apply it equally to **IDPs**, who are the majority of the displaced.

#### Possible resolution ideas

- A minimum MHPSS funding share in every UNHCR appeal.

- Scale up WHO and UNHCR non-specialist programmes through training of community workers.

- MHPSS pledges at the 2027 Global Refugee Forum.

- Better data: include mental health indicators in UNHCR needs assessments.

- Protect refugees' right to work and study as a mental health measure.

#### Tough questions to expect

- **"With UNHCR only a third funded, why should money go to mental health instead of food and shelter?"** Because they are not alternatives: the IASC pyramid starts with basic services delivered with dignity, and community-based MHPSS is cheap. Untreated trauma undermines everything else, from children's learning to people's ability to work and return home.

- **"Aren't Western trauma models unsuitable in Africa?"** That is exactly Mozambique's point: support must be community-based and culturally grounded, as in our reintegration of child soldiers after the civil war, while still offering clinical care to the few who need it.

- **"Does Mozambique have the capacity to deliver this?"** Not alone. It has very few specialists, which is why it relies on task-shifting and why international cooperation and predictable funding matter.

- **"What can UNHCR do that WHO can't?"** UNHCR is in the camps, settlements and border points; it can build MHPSS into protection, registration and community work, while WHO sets clinical standards.

## Topic: protection along migration routes

**UNHCR topic 2:** "Strengthening Protection for Refugees and Asylum Seekers along Migration Routes". The focus is on people **on the move**: before they reach a country that can grant asylum.

#### Definitions

- **Mixed movements**: refugees, asylum seekers and migrants travelling together, on the same routes, using the same smugglers, facing the same dangers, but with different legal rights.

- **Refugee versus migrant**: a refugee is protected by the 1951 Convention and must not be returned to danger; a migrant moves for other reasons but still has human rights. "Asylum seeker" means someone whose claim has not yet been decided.

- **Smuggling versus trafficking** (the 2000 Palermo Protocols to the UN Convention against Transnational Organized Crime): **smuggling** is a paid service to cross a border illegally, a crime against the state, and it ends at the border; **trafficking** is exploitation through force, fraud or coercion, a crime against the person, and it can happen without crossing a border.

- **Non-refoulement**: the ban on returning anyone to persecution or serious harm (1951 Convention Art. 33; Convention against Torture Art. 3). It is customary international law, so it binds even states that have not ratified the Refugee Convention, and it applies at the border, not only inside the country.

- **Pushback**: forcing people back across a border without assessing their claims; a breach of non-refoulement.

- **Externalisation**: rich states paying or partnering with transit states to stop people before they arrive.

- **Route-based approach**: UNHCR's strategy (with IOM) of offering protection, information and alternatives all along a route, in countries of origin, transit and destination, instead of only at the end.

#### The main routes and their dangers

| Route | Key facts |
| --- | --- |
| **Central Mediterranean** (Libya and Tunisia to Italy) | The deadliest sea route; tens of thousands dead or missing since 2014 (IOM Missing Migrants Project). The 2023 Pylos shipwreck off Greece killed about 600. Detention and abuse in Libya. |
| **Atlantic route** (West Africa to the Canary Islands) | Surged in 2023–24; long, deadly journeys in open boats |
| **Sahara crossings** | UNHCR, IOM and the Mixed Migration Centre warn more people may die crossing the desert than the sea |
| **Eastern route** (Horn of Africa to Yemen and Saudi Arabia) | Boat sinkings off Yemen; a 2023 Human Rights Watch report accused Saudi border guards of killing migrants |
| **Southern route** (Horn of Africa and Great Lakes to South Africa) | Through Kenya, Tanzania, **Malawi, Mozambique**, Zambia and Zimbabwe; almost entirely smuggler-run and understudied. Your route. |
| **Darién Gap** (Colombia to Panama) | Record of over 500,000 crossings in 2023, then collapsed in 2025 after US and Panamanian policy changes |
| Andaman Sea and Bay of Bengal | Rohingya boat journeys from Myanmar and Bangladesh |

#### Legal and policy framework

- **1951 Convention**: non-refoulement (Art. 33), no penalties for illegal entry (Art. 31).

- **Law of the sea**: the duty to rescue people in distress at sea (UNCLOS Art. 98, the SOLAS and SAR conventions).

- **Palermo Protocols (2000)** on smuggling and trafficking.

- **OAU Refugee Convention (1969)** and the **Kampala Convention** (2009) for Africa.

- **Global Compact on Refugees** and **Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration** (both 2018). The migration compact's Objective 8 commits states to save lives and look for missing migrants.

- Regional policies: the EU Pact on Migration and Asylum (2024, applying from June 2026); externalisation deals such as EU–Türkiye (2016), Italy–Libya (2017), EU–Tunisia (2023) and Italy–Albania (2023). The UK–Rwanda plan was scrapped in 2024.

#### Main debates

- **Border control versus access to asylum**: states' right to control borders against the duty not to push people back.

- **Externalisation**: does it save lives or just move the danger to Libya or the Sahara?

- **Search and rescue**: state-run rescue versus criminalising NGO rescue ships.

- **Safe and legal pathways** (resettlement, humanitarian visas, family reunification, labour mobility) as the real alternative to smugglers.

- **Burden-sharing**: transit states in the Global South carry the risk while funding falls.

#### Mozambique on the southern route

- Migrants from **Ethiopia and Somalia**, and refugees from the **Great Lakes**, travel south through Tanzania and Malawi, cross into Mozambique (often in Tete or Niassa) and head for **South Africa**. IOM recorded about **33,700 movements from Mozambique to South Africa in 2025**, mostly Mozambicans and Malawians.

- **The Tete tragedy, 24 March 2020:** police stopped a truck coming from Malawi and found **64 Ethiopian men dead from suffocation** in a sealed container; 14 survived. The Mozambican driver admitted he had been paid to smuggle them. Similar finds followed in the region: a mass grave of Ethiopian migrants in northern Malawi (2022) and bodies dumped near Lusaka, Zambia (2022).

- **Mozambicans as asylum seekers:** people fleeing Cabo Delgado tried to cross into **Tanzania**, which **forcibly returned over 10,000 of them in 2021** through the Negomano border post. UNHCR called this refoulement. Mozambique knows from its own citizens why non-refoulement matters.

- **Returns from South Africa:** Mozambicans deported from South Africa, and xenophobic violence there, are a running concern.

- Mozambique has a 2008 law against trafficking in persons and works with IOM and SADC on migration.

#### Mozambique's position (how to present it)

- **Non-refoulement is absolute**, at borders and at sea; Mozambique speaks from the 2021 experience of its own citizens being pushed back.

- **Protect people, prosecute smugglers:** target smuggling and trafficking networks, never the people they move; identify trafficking victims and refugees early.

- **Regional cooperation** through SADC and the AU: shared data, joint training for border officials, the Southern African migration dialogue (MIDSA), cooperation with IOM and UNHCR.

- **Protection-sensitive border management**: screening and referral to asylum at border posts, not only security checks.

- **Safe and legal pathways** and labour mobility in southern Africa as alternatives to smugglers.

- **Fair burden-sharing:** transit and host states in Africa need predictable funding; the closure of UNHCR's Southern Africa bureau shows the cost of donor cuts.

- Applies the African definitions: the OAU Convention's wider refugee definition and the Kampala Convention.

#### Possible resolution ideas

- Expand UNHCR's route-based approach to the southern route, with protection points in Malawi, Mozambique and Zimbabwe.

- Regional SADC protocol on search for missing migrants and identification of the dead.

- Training and referral systems at border posts, funded through the Global Compact.

- More resettlement and complementary pathways from transit countries.

- Independent monitoring of pushbacks.

#### Tough questions to expect

- **"Mozambique has reservations to the Refugee Convention, including on freedom of movement. Isn't that hypocritical?"** In practice Mozambique applies them flexibly and many refugees live in cities; it is reviewing its framework with UNHCR, and it adopted an IDP policy and ratified the Kampala Convention. Concede, then pivot.

- **"Should states be allowed to stop migrants at borders?"** States can manage borders, but every person must be able to ask for asylum and must not be pushed back to danger.

- **"What should UNHCR do about smuggling? It isn't a police agency."** UNHCR identifies and protects refugees within mixed flows, gives information about risks, and advocates for pathways; law enforcement belongs to UNODC and states.

## Interview: your pitch, NMUN rules and situations

This year's guidelines add "the ability to navigate **situational questions** based on potential delegate experiences and challenges at NMUN". These test judgement, and whether you understand how NMUN actually works. Every answer should show four things: **stay in role, stay diplomatic, use NMUN procedure correctly, aim for consensus.**

#### Your pitch (60 seconds, learn it)

> Example answer: "I chose Mozambique because it is a country that has lived almost every issue on the agenda: liberation, civil war, a UN-backed peace, and now an insurgency that has displaced more than a million people. In GA1 I would represent a state that hired Wagner and a South African contractor and learned why private military companies need binding rules. In UNHCR I would represent a country of origin, transit and asylum on the southern route, and a long-standing member of UNHCR's Executive Committee. I may be new to MUN, but I prepare carefully and I listen, and I think that is what Mozambique's delegation needs."

#### How NMUN-NY works (know this cold)

- **The conference:** National Model United Nations, New York, every spring, with thousands of university students. Committees meet over several days, partly in UN Headquarters. Your university's delegation represents one country (Mozambique) across all its committees.

- **The Dais:** each committee is run by a **Director** and **Assistant Director** (NMUN Secretariat staff), sometimes with a student **Chair**. The Dais can rule out motions that are dilatory or disruptive.

- **Before:** the **Background Guide** for each committee, and a **position paper** per committee (your country's policy on both topics). Position papers can win awards.

- **No pre-written resolutions.** NMUN does not accept them: any clause produced by one person, or with AI, without being discussed and negotiated in a working group counts as pre-written. You can bring ideas, not finished clauses.

- **Philosophy:** NMUN uses the real rules of procedure of each UN body as closely as possible and stresses the **sovereign equality** of states. Diplomacy and consensus are rewarded, not domination.

#### NMUN's rules of procedure (the parts you'll be asked about)

| Item | NMUN rule |
| --- | --- |
| Quorum | Debate can open with one-third of members present (Rule 20); a **majority** must be present to take decisions |
| Setting the agenda | First business. A speakers list opens; a motion to set the agenda in a given order passes by simple majority. If no decision within about **90 minutes**, the Secretariat sets it in Background Guide order. |
| Speakers list | Formal debate (Rule 26). You can be on the list only once at a time; re-add yourself after speaking. Speaking time is set by the Secretariat and **cannot be changed in the first session**. When the list runs out, debate closes and voting begins. |
| Informal debate | A **motion to suspend the meeting** for a set time (Rule 28; simple majority, no stated purpose). This is where negotiation and drafting happen. **There are no "moderated" or "unmoderated caucuses" at NMUN.** |
| Points | **Only points of order** (Rule 24), to correct a procedural error by the Dais, never interrupting a speech. **Points of personal privilege, inquiry and information do not exist at NMUN.** Practical questions go to the Dais during a suspension. |
| Working papers | Need **sponsors and signatories equal to 20% of the committee** (Rule 33). Sponsors are the main authors; signatories just want it debated. Sponsors distribute working papers themselves. **Don't name a working paper in a formal speech**; discuss its ideas. |
| Draft resolutions | Once the Dais approves a working paper it becomes a draft resolution, belongs to the whole committee, and the sponsor and signatory names are removed. It can now be named in speeches. |
| Amendments | **Friendly** (all sponsors agree) are added automatically. **Unfriendly** ones are debated (two for, two against, 15-second speeches) and voted on, furthest from the original first. Amendments can change **operative clauses only**, never preambular ones. |
| Closure of debate | Two speakers against, then a **two-thirds** vote (Rule 31); the committee moves straight to voting |
| Adjournment of debate | Ends the topic without a vote ("tabling"): two for, two against, simple majority (Rule 30). Reopening needs a two-thirds vote to reconsider (Rule 35). |
| Order of motions | Suspend the meeting → adjourn the meeting → adjourn debate → close debate (Rule 32) |
| Procedural votes | Everyone votes, including observers; **no abstaining** on procedural votes |
| Substantive votes | Members only; simple majority of those voting yes or no. "Present and voting" at roll call means you **cannot abstain**. Roll call: yes, no, abstain or pass (a pass must become yes or no). |
| Adoption by acclamation | If nobody requests a recorded vote, a draft is adopted without a vote. Over 80% of UN resolutions are adopted by consensus. |
| Division of the proposal | Vote separately on parts of a draft (Rule 47): two for, two against, simple majority; the most disruptive division goes first |
| Voting procedure | Doors close; no talking, no leaving and re-entering; no breaks until finished. Drafts are voted in the order the Dais approved them. |
| Right of reply | Only when a remark impugns your **state's** sovereignty or government; requested **in writing** to the Secretariat, read by the President (Rule 27) |
| Explanation of vote | Only for sponsors who vote against their own draft after an unfriendly amendment or division; submitted in writing (Rule 45) |

#### Situations: working with others

| Situation | Strong answer |
| --- | --- |
| Your bloc (the African Group) ignores your ideas | During a suspension, approach individual delegates, find one ally, and offer concrete language tied to the bloc's shared interest (for example, capacity-building for developing states). Bring a draft idea, not a speech. |
| A powerful delegation dominates a working group | Don't fight publicly. Offer to draft a specific section, gather signatories, and propose merging groups. Every delegation is equal at the UN. |
| Someone copies your idea into their working paper | Raise it privately, propose co-sponsorship or merging. The goal is a strong resolution, not credit. Once a draft is approved, names are removed anyway. |
| Two working groups with similar papers refuse to merge | Map the overlap, propose a merged text keeping each group's key clauses, and involve the Dais if duplication stalls the committee. |
| A delegate brings clauses written beforehand (or by AI) | Remind them politely that NMUN doesn't accept pre-written clauses; suggest the group negotiate the ideas together. If it continues, inform the Dais. |
| Your delegation partner disagrees with you | Settle it privately and quickly, decide by country policy, and never contradict each other in committee. Split roles: one speaks, one drafts. |
| A delegate is quiet and excluded from your group | Invite them in and ask for their country's view. Inclusion builds consensus and is exactly what NMUN rewards. |
| A delegate behaves rudely or insults your country | Stay courteous. If a remark impugns Mozambique's sovereignty, submit a written right of reply; for misconduct, speak to the Dais. |
| A delegate interrupts speeches with points | Don't escalate. Points of order can't interrupt speeches; the Dais handles it. |

#### Situations: your country's positions

| Situation | Strong answer |
| --- | --- |
| Mozambique's policy conflicts with your personal opinion | Represent Mozambique faithfully; that is the job. Look for language within its policy that moves the debate forward. |
| A clause you like goes against Mozambique's interests | Propose wording that keeps the goal but protects the interest (for example, "encourages" rather than "demands"; voluntary rather than mandatory). |
| Another delegate attacks Mozambique for hiring Wagner | Acknowledge it calmly and turn it into the argument: that experience is exactly why Mozambique wants binding rules. |
| Someone says Mozambique has reservations to the Refugee Convention | Acknowledge, explain flexible practice (urban refugees, local integration, statelessness accession, Kampala Convention), and pivot to burden-sharing. |
| Your bloc's line differs from Mozambique's national interest | Mozambique usually aligns with the African Group; where it differs, speak in your national capacity ("aligns with the African Group and adds the following"). |
| A question comes up that you didn't prepare | Reason from the committee's mandate and Mozambique's principles (sovereignty, African ownership, development, burden-sharing); never invent facts. Check with your partner during suspension. |

#### Situations: procedure and drafting

| Situation | Strong answer |
| --- | --- |
| Your working paper doesn't have enough signatories | You need 20% of the committee: lobby during suspension, accept reasonable changes, or merge with a similar paper. |
| An unfriendly amendment guts your draft resolution | Speak against it in the two-against slots, rally votes; if it passes and you then vote against your own draft, submit a written explanation of vote. |
| Someone moves to divide your draft resolution | Decide which clauses matter most, speak against the division if needed, and make sure supporters vote to keep the key operative clauses. |
| The committee wants to close debate before your paper is approved | Speak against closure (two speakers against are allowed; it needs two-thirds), explaining that more proposals deserve consideration. |
| Consensus is impossible on a clause | Narrow the disagreement, soften the verb, split the clause, or accept a recorded vote. Over 80% of UN resolutions are adopted by consensus, so try first. |
| Your clause goes beyond the committee's mandate | Rewrite it within the mandate: GA1 "recommends" and "calls upon"; UNHCR's governing body requests the High Commissioner and encourages states, but neither can bind states or authorise force. |
| You realise mid-conference you've misunderstood a rule | Ask the Dais during a suspension, never during formal debate. |
| Voting procedure takes hours and you need to leave | You can't re-enter once you leave during voting; plan breaks before it starts. |
