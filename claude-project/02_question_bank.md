# NMUN 2027 selection interview: question bank

Each question has likely follow-ups and the points a strong answer must hit. Panels rephrase questions, so the coach should vary the wording. Areas match the guidelines: A UN core, B committee, C topics, D country, E applying policy, plus Personal and Current events.

---

## Personal and motivation

**P1. Tell us about yourself.**
- Follow-ups: What's your major and how does it connect to the UN? What did you do in MUN before?
- Must hit: 45–60 seconds; major and one genuine interest; why international affairs; one strength relevant to NMUN (research, writing, listening). Ends with commitment.

**P2. Why do you want to go to NMUN specifically?**
- Follow-ups: What do you know about how NMUN differs from other conferences? What would you do if you're not selected?
- Must hit: NMUN rewards realistic diplomacy, consensus-building and staying in character, not domination; it's the largest and most UN-faithful conference; representing the university; specific personal goal (learning negotiation, public speaking).

**P3. You have very little MUN experience. Why should we pick you?**
- Follow-ups: How do we know you'll keep up the work for five months? What have you done in the last two weeks?
- Must hit: concrete proof of preparation (what you studied, how), fast learner, coachable, no bad habits to unlearn, will put in the hours.

**P4. What is your biggest weakness as a delegate?**
- Follow-ups: Give an example. What have you done about it so far?
- Must hit: real but manageable (e.g. public speaking nerves); a system you're using to fix it (daily timed practice, mock interviews); how you use strengths meanwhile (drafting, research).

**P5. Describe a time you worked in a team with people who disagreed.**
- Follow-ups: What did you personally do? What would you do differently?
- Must hit: specific situation, your action (listened, found shared goal, divided work), result. STAR structure.

**P6. A delegate in your bloc is dominating and ignoring others. What do you do?**
- Follow-ups: What if they're from a much more powerful country? What if they refuse to cooperate?
- Must hit: diplomacy, not confrontation; acknowledge their ideas; divide drafting work; bring in quiet delegates; private conversation; focus on getting sponsors and votes.

**P7. Which committee did you choose and why?**
- Follow-ups: What if you get your second choice? What do you find hardest about that committee?
- Must hit: one reason about the topics, one about your country's role there, one about your skills. Positive about the second choice too.

**P8. Do you have any questions for us?**
- Must hit: have 1–2 genuine questions ready (e.g. "What separated the strongest delegates last year?", "How is training structured between now and March?").

## A. UN history, structure and functions

**A1. Why did the League of Nations fail, and what did the UN learn from it?**
- Follow-ups: Give one specific crisis. Did the UN actually solve the problem of great powers?
- Must hit: unanimity rule; great powers absent (US never joined; Germany, Japan, Italy left; USSR expelled 1939); no enforcement; Manchuria 1931 and Abyssinia 1935. UN fixes: majority voting, P5 veto keeps great powers in, Chapter VII enforcement, Article 2(4).

**A2. Walk us through how the UN was created.**
- Follow-ups: Why is 24 October UN Day? Who were the "Four Policemen" and why was France added?
- Must hit: Atlantic Charter (Aug 1941), Declaration by United Nations (1 Jan 1942, 26 states), Moscow (1943), Dumbarton Oaks (1944), Yalta (Feb 1945, veto), San Francisco (Apr–Jun 1945, 50 states, signed 26 June), in force 24 Oct 1945, 51 original members (Poland).

**A3. What are the purposes and principles of the UN?**
- Follow-ups: How do Articles 2(4) and 2(7) conflict with R2P? Which principle matters most to small states?
- Must hit: Article 1 (peace and security, friendly relations and self-determination, cooperation and human rights, harmonizing centre). Article 2: 2(1) sovereign equality, 2(3) peaceful settlement, 2(4) no force, 2(7) non-intervention except Chapter VII.

**A4. Name the six principal organs. Which is most powerful?**
- Follow-ups: Then why does the GA matter? Is the Trusteeship Council useless?
- Must hit: GA, SC, ECOSOC, Trusteeship Council (inactive since 1994), ICJ, Secretariat. SC most powerful legally (Art. 25, Chapter VII). GA: universal, budget, elections, Uniting for Peace.

**A5. Are GA resolutions binding? Is anything the GA does binding?**
- Follow-ups: Give an example of a GA resolution that became customary law. How do states use non-binding resolutions?
- Must hit: generally recommendations; binding on internal matters (budget Art. 17, elections); can become evidence of custom (state practice + opinio juris), e.g. UDHR.

**A6. Explain the Security Council's membership and voting.**
- Follow-ups: Does an abstention count as a veto? Who's on the Council now? Who joins in 2027?
- Must hit: 15 members, P5 + 10 elected for 2 years by two-thirds of GA, regional split (Africa 3, Asia-Pacific 2, GRULAC 2, WEOG 2, EEG 1); 9 votes, no P5 negative vote on substantive matters; abstention not a veto. 2027 newcomers: Austria, Kyrgyzstan, Portugal, Trinidad and Tobago, Zimbabwe.

**A7. What can the Security Council actually do?**
- Follow-ups: Difference between Chapter VI and VII? Example of Article 41 and Article 42?
- Must hit: Chapter VI non-binding recommendations; Chapter VII binding: Art. 39 determine threat, Art. 41 sanctions, Art. 42 force ("all necessary means"); peacekeeping; ICC referrals (Darfur 2005, Libya 2011); tribunals (ICTY, ICTR).

**A8. What happens when the Security Council is paralysed by a veto?**
- Follow-ups: First and latest use of Uniting for Peace? Is it binding?
- Must hit: Uniting for Peace (A/RES/377 (V), 1950), emergency special sessions, recommendations only; first used Suez 1956; Ukraine 2022; veto initiative A/RES/76/262 (debate within 10 working days).

**A9. Should the Security Council be reformed? How?**
- Follow-ups: Why hasn't it happened? What does Africa want?
- Must hit: G4 (Brazil, Germany, India, Japan), Uniting for Consensus (Italy, Pakistan…), Ezulwini Consensus (2 permanent with veto + 5 non-permanent for Africa); IGN process; needs Charter amendment ratified by all P5 (Art. 108); Pact for the Future commitment.

**A10. How is the Secretary-General chosen, and what's happening now?**
- Follow-ups: What powers does the SG have? Has there been a woman SG?
- Must hit: nominations, GA dialogues, SC straw polls, SC recommendation (no veto), GA appointment; 5-year term; Art. 97 and 99; Guterres's term ends 31 Dec 2026; current race status (check news); no woman SG yet; regional rotation argument for Latin America.

**A11. Name a few Secretaries-General and what they're known for.**
- Must hit: Hammarskjöld (UNEF, died 1961), U Thant (first non-European), Boutros-Ghali (Egypt, Agenda for Peace, US veto of second term), Annan (MDGs, Nobel), Ban (SDGs, Paris), Guterres.

**A12. How does a state join the UN? Why aren't Taiwan, Kosovo or Palestine full members?**
- Follow-ups: Can the UN recognize a state? What changed for Palestine in 2024?
- Must hit: Art. 4: application, SC recommendation (9, no veto), GA two-thirds; UN doesn't recognize states; Taiwan (Res. 2758, 1971); Kosovo (Russia/China opposition); Palestine observer state 2012, A/RES/ES-10/23 upgraded rights 2024, US veto of membership April 2024.

**A13. What is ECOSOC and why does it matter?**
- Follow-ups: Name its functional and regional commissions. What is the HLPF?
- Must hit: 54 members, 3-year terms; coordination of economic, social, environmental work and specialized agencies; NGO consultative status (Art. 71); functional commissions (CSW, CPD, CCPCJ, CND…); five regional commissions; HLPF and VNRs.

**A14. What is the ICJ? Difference with the ICC?**
- Follow-ups: Can the ICJ force a state to appear? Name a current case.
- Must hit: principal judicial organ, The Hague, 15 judges, 9 years, elected by GA and SC; contentious (states, consent, binding) vs advisory (GA/SC/agencies, non-binding); ICC tries individuals (Rome Statute); South Africa v. Israel; climate advisory opinion July 2025.

**A15. What are the UN's biggest successes and failures?**
- Follow-ups: Is the UN still relevant? What's the single biggest reform needed?
- Must hit: balanced. Successes: no great-power war, decolonization, human rights system, peacekeeping (Nobel 1988), smallpox, Montreal Protocol, humanitarian work. Failures: Rwanda, Srebrenica, Syria/Ukraine/Gaza veto paralysis, unrepresentative SC, funding dependence. End with reform.

**A16. How is the UN funded? What is the liquidity crisis?**
- Must hit: assessed (regular and peacekeeping budgets, scale of assessments, 22% ceiling) vs voluntary; Fifth Committee; late/unpaid contributions, US cuts; UN80 Initiative.

**A17. What are the SDGs? Are they on track?**
- Follow-ups: How do they differ from the MDGs? Who monitors them?
- Must hit: A/RES/70/1 (2015), 17 goals, 169 targets, universal; MDGs 2000–2015; HLPF + VNRs; ~15% on track (2023); SDG Summit 2027; financing gap; FfD4 Sevilla 2025.

**A18. What is R2P? Does it work?**
- Follow-ups: Why are many states suspicious of it? Is it law?
- Must hit: 2005 World Summit (paras 138–139); four crimes; three pillars; Rwanda/Srebrenica origins; Libya 2011 controversy; political commitment not treaty; France–Mexico, ACT Code of Conduct.

**A19. What are the sources of international law? What is jus cogens?**
- Must hit: ICJ Statute Art. 38(1): treaties, custom (practice + opinio juris), general principles, judicial decisions and writings; jus cogens = peremptory norms (aggression, genocide, torture, slavery…), no persistent objector, conflicting treaties void (VCLT Art. 53).

**A20. Difference between signing and ratifying a treaty? What is a reservation?**
- Must hit: signature = intent + don't defeat object and purpose (VCLT Art. 18); ratification = consent to be bound; accession; reservations allowed unless incompatible with object and purpose (CEDAW example).

**A21. Difference between peacekeeping and peacebuilding?**
- Must hit: Agenda for Peace (1992) four tools; peacekeeping principles (consent, impartiality, minimal force); peacebuilding = root causes, institutions; PBC (2005, 31 members, advisory, consensus), PBF; transitions (MINUSMA, UNIFIL ending 2026).

**A22. Difference between IHL and international human rights law?**
- Must hit: IHL only in armed conflict, binds all parties, Geneva Conventions; IHRL always, binds states, UDHR/covenants; both apply in war.

## B. Committee mandate, powers and limits
Use these for whichever committees are in my profile. If none chosen yet, ask them about any committee from `03_nmun_2027_committees.md`.

**B1. What is your committee's mandate?**
- Follow-ups: What document created it? When? What is it NOT allowed to do?
- Must hit: founding document and year; mandate in one sentence; who it reports to; what kind of outcome it produces (binding? recommendations? agreed conclusions?).

**B2. Who sits on your committee and how are decisions made?**
- Must hit: membership size and how members are elected or whether it's universal; voting rule (simple majority, consensus, two-thirds); when and where it meets.

**B3. What are its subsidiary bodies and key partners?**
- Follow-ups: How does it work with [related body]? Which agency would implement your proposals?
- Must hit: subsidiaries and the agencies/funds that implement its recommendations (e.g. GA3 ↔ HRC, OHCHR, UNHCR; CSW ↔ UN Women; PBC ↔ PBSO, PBF).

**B4. What has the committee achieved? What are its limits?**
- Must hit: one concrete past outcome; honest limit (non-binding, consensus slows it, funding).

**B5. If your committee adopts a resolution, what actually happens next?**
- Must hit: which body adopts it (e.g. GA Main Committees → plenary), who implements, reporting back by SG/agency, funding via Fifth Committee for new costs.

**B6. How does your committee relate to the Security Council / HRC / ECOSOC?**
- Must hit: the relationship and the boundary (e.g. GA can't recommend on disputes SC is handling, Art. 12; PBC advises both GA and SC).

## C. Topics (definitions, reports, resolutions)
For each of my topics, rotate through these.

**C1. Define [key term of the topic].**
- Must hit: a precise definition, ideally the one used in the main UN instrument.

**C2. How big is the problem? Give numbers.**
- Must hit: 1–2 credible statistics with source (UN agency report).

**C3. What has the UN already done on this topic?**
- Follow-ups: Name the most recent resolution. Which report should we read first?
- Must hit: timeline of 3–5 key frameworks/resolutions/reports with names and years; the latest one.

**C4. What are the main disagreements between member states on this topic?**
- Must hit: the 2–3 fault lines (e.g. binding vs voluntary, sovereignty vs monitoring, funding North vs South) and which blocs hold which side.

**C5. What are the gaps in the current framework?**
- Must hit: implementation, enforcement, funding, data, coverage.

**C6. Propose a realistic solution the committee could adopt.**
- Follow-ups: Who pays? Who implements? How would you measure success? Would it pass?
- Must hit: within mandate, builds on existing bodies, funding source, monitoring/reporting mechanism.

**C7. How does this topic connect to the SDGs?**
- Must hit: the specific goal and target numbers.

## D. Country
**D1. Describe your country's geography and why it matters for its foreign policy.**
- Must hit: location, neighbours, strategic features, resources, climate exposure, and how each drives a priority.

**D2. What are your country's main disputes or tensions right now?**
- Follow-ups: What is the UN involved in? What's your country's official position?
- Must hit: 2–3 issues with the official position and UN angle (mission, resolution, court case).

**D3. Which groups and blocs does your country belong to?**
- Must hit: UN regional group, regional organization, G77/NAM/OIC/Arab League/AU/OAS etc., and why they matter in negotiations.

**D4. What are your country's foreign policy principles?**
- Must hit: stated doctrine in its own words (latest GA General Debate speech), with an example of it in action.

**D5. What is your country's record at the UN?**
- Must hit: SC terms, HRC membership, peacekeeping contributions, posts held, conferences hosted, key treaties ratified or rejected.

**D6. Who is your country's head of state, foreign minister and UN ambassador?**

## E. Applying the country's policy
**E1. What is your country's position on [topic]?**
- Follow-ups: How do you know? Show me a vote or statement. Why does it hold that position?
- Must hit: stance in the country's voice, the interest or principle behind it, evidence (law, programme, vote, statement).

**E2. How would your country vote on a resolution that [binding mechanism / strong monitoring / condemnation]?**
- Must hit: reasoned prediction from interests and allies; what amendment it would seek.

**E3. Who would your country work with in committee, and who would it oppose?**
- Must hit: specific allies/blocs and why; opponents and the point of conflict.

**E4. Your country's position conflicts with a major power. How do you negotiate?**
- Must hit: stay in character; find overlapping interests; softer operative language ("encourages"); work through blocs; respectful tone.

**E5. What national programme or law would you showcase as a model on [topic]?**
- Must hit: a real, named national initiative and what it achieved.

**E6. A delegate accuses your country of hypocrisy on [topic]. Respond.**
- Must hit: calm, no personal attack, acknowledge challenges, cite actions taken, redirect to cooperation (right-of-reply style).

**E7. Give us 60 seconds as your country's delegate opening on [topic].**
- Must hit: hook, stance, evidence, one proposal, call to cooperation.
